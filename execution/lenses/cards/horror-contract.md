# LENS: Horror Genre Contract
id: horror-contract
source: skills/horror-screenwriter
applies_to: both
role: genre_contract
genres: horror

## What this lens evaluates
The studio bar: **horror must be REALLY SCARY.** Your job is to locate the scares ON THE PAGE — page numbers where you actually felt dread, tension, or shock while reading — and to test the machinery that produces them (Truby's horror beats, scare mechanics, monster design). Craft is judged only insofar as it serves fear.

## Criteria
- **Scares located, not assumed**: mark every sequence where the pages themselves generate dread/fear/unease, where attention wandered, and what image lingers after reading. Horror Equation: fear + shock + disgust → anxiety → engagement.
- **Scare sequence architecture**: each major scare should run expectation → tension (delay, delay, delay) → misdirection → payoff (or deliberate withholding) → character reaction (the mirroring effect — shown fear is what the audience feels).
- **Scare variety and taxonomy**: earned jump scares (Lewton Bus), slow-burn reveals, dramatic-irony dread, visual and intellectual disgust, a fundamental shock reserved for the ending. Name each set piece — the script needs at least one memorable one (the clapping game, the hallway crawl).
- **Dread escalation**: steady-ramp or slow-burn, but every encounter worse than the last, valleys between peaks, and a **cyclone effect** climax — nightmares sequenced faster and worse. No front-loaded best scare, no flat repetition.
- **Truby beats**: the **Ghost** (sin of the past that drives WHY the monster attacks THIS person), the **Haunted House** (setting expressing the hero's psychology — cellar/attic logic, not interchangeable backdrop), monster attacks, hero as victim, apparent defeat, battle, moral decision, new equilibrium.
- **Monster/threat design**: consistent rules never broken; a weakness giving the hero a fighting chance; revelation staged (evidence → glimpse → confirmation → understanding → confrontation), never a "coupon shot" reveal without buildup; prey reversal (it puts humans back in the food chain); unpredictable, amoral, beyond control.
- **Monster as Double**: the monster externalizes the hero's psychological/moral flaw; confronting it means confronting the self.
- **Transcendence (bonus, never substitute)**: does it ask "what is human" — social themes organic to the premise, possibly a structural flip (apparent monster reveals humanity, humans reveal monstrousness)? Commentary without earned scares fails.

## What to cite
Page numbers of every scare that landed (and the stretch where nothing did); the beat establishing the Ghost; the monster's rules and any page where they break; the staging of the first full reveal; one set piece quoted beat by beat showing tension build and payoff; the escalation map across attacks; the passage where the setting expresses the hero's psychology.

## Red flags
- Spring-loaded-cat fake-outs; unearned jump scares; the coupon-shot monster reveal.
- Same attack beat repeated; constant intensity with no valleys; biggest scare in act one.
- No Ghost — no reason this monster attacks this person; no Double.
- Monster over-explained early, rules inconsistent, no weakness, generic design.
- Interchangeable setting; "slow burn" that is actually nothing happening for 30 pages.
- Scares that punctuate rather than advance story; no character reactions after scares.

## Grading
- **Strong**: multiple scares that genuinely land on the read, varied and escalating to a cyclone-effect climax; Ghost, Double, and staged reveal all working; at least one signature set piece.
- **Solid**: real dread in stretches, machinery mostly sound, but scares repeat, escalation flattens, or the monster's connection to the hero is thin.
- **Weak / FAILS THE CONTRACT**: a structurally competent script that is not scary FAILS this lens — clean acts, functional character work, and zero pages that raise the pulse is a failing horror script, full stop. Say so plainly.

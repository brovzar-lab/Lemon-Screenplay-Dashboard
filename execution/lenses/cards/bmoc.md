# LENS: BMOC Beat Engineering

applies_to: both

## What this lens evaluates
Peter Russell's BMOC (Beginning, Middle, Obstacle, Climax) scene mechanics: every scene as a mini-war that turns and stings. Apply to sampled scenes — at minimum the protagonist-establishing scene, the midpoint, and the genre's core event — plus any scene flagged as flat.

## Criteria
- **The beat is a mini-war.** Four required elements: a hero of the beat (whoever has the active objective now — not automatically the protagonist), an antagonist actively blocking, an actual clash of intentions (not vibes), and a **winner and loser**. No shift in advantage = a holding pattern, not a beat.
- **Beat question.** Every beat has a binary Yes/No question a tired viewer at 1 AM could track ("Will Jesse sell the meth?"). If it cannot be phrased as Yes/No, that IS the diagnosis. The best questions are binary on the surface and character-defining underneath ("Will she get the files without betraying someone?").
- **Four crescendos.** B (~25% in), M (~50%), O (~75%, worst complication), C (near end, final answer + price paid) — each answering the SAME beat question Yes or No. Four mini-endings, not one. Identify the answer pattern and its emotional shape (Yes-Yes-No-Yes = comeback; No-No-No-Yes = against all odds; Yes-No-No-No = collapse; Yes-Yes-No-No = trap closes).
- **BMOC points are CHOICES, not information.** At each turn the hero must decide, commit, reveal, concede, bluff, sacrifice, or change tactic. Points where information merely arrives read as exposition. At least one choice should be morally compromising or identity-threatening.
- **Turns are observable.** A line lands, a door closes, a weapon appears, a confession drops, a price is named. Internal-only turns don't play.
- **Suspense tools present:** a **ticking clock** that genuinely constrains choices — the best are social or psychological (someone could walk in, a mask is slipping, a lie has 30 seconds left), not just timers; **good news / bad news oscillation**, at least 3 swings inside the beat; **stakes that escalate** — more cost, fewer exits, a moral line approaching — often mutating the beat away from its original objective; **surprise and reversal from character strategy or wound**, never from random events.
- **Dialogue tactics.** Each volley uses a different tactic: charm, deflection, accusation, moral claim, threat, humiliation, feigned vulnerability, sudden honesty. No tactic change = just talking.
- **Split beats.** Cutting away before O/C is legitimate if the aftermath delivers a bigger emotional truth and the logic stays reconstructable; withholding just to withhold is a cheat.
- **Core wound pressure.** Where a wound is established, the strongest beats press it — the O point forces the hero to confront or betray it; the best reversals are identity crystallizations ("I didn't know she'd go that far"), not plot twists.
- **Length.** Premium-TV beats run ~1.5–3 pages; over ~4 pages is usually two beats stitched together, each owed its own BMOC. Pre-beat runway (setup before the contest ignites) is allowed but brief — the mini-war must ignite fast.

## What to cite
Per sampled scene: the beat question as a Yes/No sentence; the page/line of each B, M, O, C turn and its answer; the choice at each turn; the ticking clock; the GN/BN swings; the reversal and its source; who won and lost the beat; the transition hook to the next beat.

## Red flags
The ten failure modes: mushy beat question; passive antagonist (inconvenience, not conflict); no power shift; missing or decorative ticking clock; stakes flat from start to end; BMOC points that deliver information instead of forcing choices; split beat as a cheat; antagonist with no leverage or credible threat (cannot say "no" and make it stick); no tactic changes in dialogue; surprise from coincidence rather than character.

## Grading
- **Strong:** sampled scenes each carry a trackable binary question answered at four escalating turns, every turn is a visible choice, power shifts hands, clocks and oscillations are organic, reversals character-driven.
- **Solid:** the mini-war is real but under-engineered — a soft O point, a decorative clock, tactics that repeat; fixable at scene level.
- **Weak:** scenes with no identifiable beat question, no power shift, and turns that are exposition — situation identical at entry and exit.

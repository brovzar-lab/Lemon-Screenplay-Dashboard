# LENS: Comedy Genre Contract

applies_to: both
role: genre_contract

## What this lens evaluates
The studio bar: **comedy must be HILARIOUS.** Laughter is the payload; everything else is delivery mechanism. Locate the laughs ON THE PAGE — cite the pages where you actually laughed — and test whether the script is a comedy MACHINE (a premise that generates jokes by itself) or a drama with jokes sprinkled on. Calibrated on Mexican theatrical hits (Nosotros los Nobles, Matando Cabos, No se Aceptan Devoluciones): word of mouth is how comedy wins.

## Criteria
- **Laughs located, not assumed**: page numbers where the read produced a real laugh, a smile, or nothing. Track joke density — long laugh-free stretches are the primary defect.
- **Comic engine**: does the central premise naturally generate conflict and absurdity scene after scene (the "20+ funny scenes" test), or does the writer have to invent each joke from scratch? Fish-out-of-water displacement — collision points between who the protagonist is and where they are — is the classic engine; the bigger the gap, the funnier.
- **Set pieces**: identify each major comic set piece. The script needs at least one **killer scene** — the scene people describe leaving the theater. Test it against the viral checklist: quotable, physical (works without dialogue), escalating (a building sequence, not one joke), recognition ("eso me pasó"), rewatchable.
- **Escalation**: within scenes and across the script, does the comedy heighten — each complication dumber/bigger/truer than the last — or does it plateau after the premise is established?
- **Runners and callbacks**: are jokes planted, developed, and paid off? A comedy with no runners is leaving laughs on the table; a great callback in the third act is money in the bank.
- **Joke variety and register**: mix of verbal, physical, situational, character-based humor. Identify the dominant register — satirical/system takedown, heightened absurd, heartfelt cry-comedy (laugh and gut-punch in the same beat), cringe/recognition — and whether the script commits to it rather than hedging.
- **Recognition humor / pain point**: does the comedy hit a real cultural nerve (class performance, family, bureaucracy, keeping up appearances, machismo) — the gap between who people ARE and who they PRETEND to be? Specificity drives universality (the Coco Principle).
- **Punching direction**: up/sideways at systems, power, and absurdity — not down at vulnerable people.

## What to cite
Pages where you laughed (and the longest laugh-free stretch, by page range); the best set piece quoted beat by beat showing its escalation; each runner and where it pays off; the killer-scene candidate and which viral-checklist boxes it clears; the line or image that proves the premise generates jokes on its own; any joke that depends on recognition and the pages that earn it.

## Red flags
- 10+ page stretches with no attempted laugh; jokes only in dialogue quips while situations stay flat.
- A sketch premise stretched to feature length — one joke, no escalation.
- No set piece anyone would retell; nothing quotable, nothing physical.
- Setups without payoffs; zero callbacks.
- Neutral, place-less humor a foreigner could have written; stereotype-as-punchline; dated references.
- Comedy that stops for 30 pages so the "real story" can happen.

## Grading
- **Strong**: you laughed repeatedly on the page; a genuine comic engine producing escalating set pieces; runners that pay off; at least one killer scene that clears the viral checklist.
- **Solid**: a funny premise and several scenes that land, but laughs thin out — escalation plateaus, callbacks missing, or the register wobbles.
- **Weak / FAILS THE CONTRACT**: a competent but unfunny script FAILS this lens — sound structure, likable characters, and no laughs on the page is a failing comedy, full stop. Say so plainly.

# LENS: Pluma Mexicana
id: pluma-mexicana
source: skills/pluma-mexicana
applies_to: both
role: audit
genres: all

## What this lens evaluates
Line-level craft audit for Spanish-language Mexican material: are the action lines filmable, and does the dialogue sound like real Mexican people rather than a writer — or a language model? Audits every page against the three characteristic failures: novelist's action lines, writer's dialogue, and neutral Spanish with sprinkled slang. Apply only to Spanish-language scripts; for other material, note "not applicable."

## Criteria
- **Camera-and-microphone test (action lines)**: can a camera photograph and a microphone record every sentence of description? Backstory ("desde los catorce"), interiority ("sabe que", "siente", "recuerda"), authorial intent or thesis ("es el mismo trato que..."), and adjective-soup character intros are unfilmable — novel prose, not screenplay. Strong pages convert interiority into behavior: not "she knows she shouldn't promise it" but a body that hesitates a beat too long and promises looking at the floor.
- **Writer's dialogue test**: is any line too well-made — a polished simile, an elegant antithesis, an aphorism carrying the theme INSIDE the line instead of underneath it? Real speech is mundane, trails off, repeats, digresses; a tired character does not land the perfect closer.
- **Register by age and class**: does syntax, sentence length, and verbal intelligence match the speaker? A 12-year-old does not build antithesis; a 19-year-old does not drop a complete bureaucratic simile; a 50-year-old guard does not use teen slang.
- **Sociolect, not neutral Spanish**: is the voice built from region + age + class + subculture from the first word, or is it panhispanic Spanish with "güey/chido/chafa" sprinkled on top? Could the dialogue be set in any Spanish-speaking country by changing three words? Is it anchored in the story's actual region (including border English filtering in naturally), or does it default to a generic center-of-Mexico voice?
- **Slang as grammar, not seasoning**: is slang idiomatic and correctly constructed? ("chafa" is an adjective — "está bien chafa"; "son una chafa" is wrong.) Piled-on obvious slang ("órale güey, qué chido, no manches") reads as false as neutral Spanish. Often the most Mexican line has no slang at all — just the right rhythm and syntax.
- **Specificity**: could another character say this same line? If yes, it isn't specific enough to its speaker.
- **Public vs private register**: does the same character speak differently in public (performed, sloganized) and alone (clipped, blunt, profane)? A private scene in the public voice is a miss.
- **Subtext**: characters should almost never say exactly what they mean; the theme belongs under the line.
- **Mute or near-silent characters**: their "voice" is physical vocabulary — how they hold objects, breathing, ritual, stillness — written as precise behavior.

## What to cite
Quote offending action lines verbatim and name what the camera cannot photograph in them; quote dialogue lines that are too well-made, off-register for age/class, region-less, or mis-constructed slang (with the correct construction); quote the pages where voice IS working — a line only that character could say — as the script's own benchmark; note whether public and private registers differ, with an example of each.

## Red flags
- Action lines full of memory, knowledge, feeling, intention, or theme.
- Every character speaking with the same syntax and vocabulary.
- Children or working-class characters speaking in polished literary cadence.
- Dialogue that floats in nobody's Spanish, or Spain-isms; slang used as decoration or built wrong.
- Characters announcing the theme out loud; no subtext anywhere.

## Grading
- **Strong**: action lines are shot lists with rhythm; every voice is anchored in region/age/class/subculture; slang idiomatic and calibrated; theme lives under the lines.
- **Solid**: mostly filmable and voiced, with lapses — occasional interiority in description, a few too-written lines, patches of neutral Spanish.
- **Weak**: novelistic description throughout, interchangeable voices, neutral Spanish with sprinkled or mis-built slang — pages a Mexican audience would clock as written by nobody from Mexico.

# LENS: Story Stakes
id: story-stakes
source: skills/story-stakes
applies_to: both
role: structure
genres: all

## What this lens evaluates
Whether the audience has a concrete reason to care what happens: stakes are the negative consequences of failure, the reason the hero cannot walk away. Diagnoses low tension, saggy Act 2s, and anticlimactic finales via the 11 stake types, the 8 modulating factors, and the Story Stake Matrix.

## Criteria
- **Goal and the 25% rule.** The protagonist is actively pursuing something concrete by roughly the 25% mark.
- **Stakes named per the 11 types**, each tagged and locatable on the page: 1 General protection, 2 Demise (a loved one dies — the most reliable commercial stake), 3 Livelihood, 4 Freedom, 5 Reputation, 6 Sanity, 7 Access, 8 Justice, plus the contingency stakes 9 Suffering/sacrifice, 10 Regret, 11 Hero happiness. Contingency stakes amplify but never anchor. The consequence of failure must be statable in one concrete sentence.
- **Stakes vs risk.** The stake is what the hero loses if he fails; the cost of participation is the danger he takes on by trying. A man who might be hit crossing the street has risk, not stakes — he can decline to cross. Heist trap: prison is a risk being courted, not a stake being avoided, so a second stake must do the work.
- **The Story Stake Matrix.** Axes: what is at stake — safety needs (life, protection, livelihood, freedom, sanity, home, family unit) vs spiritual needs (love, esteem, justice, standalone reputation, happiness) — against cost of participation, high vs low. Track the cell at the start of Act 2, midpoint, end of Act 2, and climax. Action/thriller/drama must reach Cell 3 (high cost, safety needs) by the climax; comedy/romance legitimately lives in Cell 1 but the great ones sneak a safety-need stake underneath.
- **Alignment (highest-value check).** Danger accepted must be warranted by what can be lost. Cell 4 (high cost for spiritual needs) is the misalignment trap — revenge stories, treasure hunts. The money rule: wealth never anchors alone.
- **The 8 modulators.** Audience–hero bond (formed by page 10, not diluted or alienated), audience–stake bond (reminders across Act 2), boundaries/restrictions (ticking clocks, depleting resources, limited powers), vulnerable populations, hero backstory (echo, shared history), setting, contingency stakes, and cost of participation. "Correct but tepid" almost always means under-modulated, not under-staked.
- **Escalation placement.** Stakes or cost rise at the midpoint (point of no return) and again just before the climax; escalation is measured in what can be lost, not in volume of destruction.
- **Climax integrity.** Something remains at stake to the end (not rescued early), the hero is still risking life, freedom, or livelihood, and the genre's promise is delivered. General→personal escalation works; personal→general late reads anticlimactic.
- **TV.** Series, season, and episode stakes exist separately; the pilot resolves episode stakes while leaving series stakes worse than they began.

## What to cite
The page/scene where each stake is established; the line proving the hero cannot walk away; the matrix cell at each act landmark with the action that places it there; the midpoint escalation beat; the moment (if any) where the stakes get rescued before the climax ends.

## Red flags
- Consequence of failure cannot be stated in one sentence.
- Hero could simply quit — risk masquerading as stakes.
- Hero risking his life for a spiritual need nobody sold (Cell 4 unsold).
- Act 2 opens with the boldest version of the plan — nowhere to escalate.
- Suffering piled on as a substitute for a reason he can't leave.
- A fifty-page-resisted decision reversed with no new stake forcing it.
- Climax with atmosphere but no live stake and no personal jeopardy.

## Grading
- **Strong:** One-sentence stakes anchored in a safety need, aligned costs, visible matrix movement to Cell 3 by the climax, several modulators turned up, stakes live to the final page.
- **Solid:** Real stakes clearly established but under-modulated or flat across Act 2 — the "technically fine but doesn't land" script.
- **Weak:** No statable consequence of failure, a hero who can walk away, misaligned danger, or a climax whose stakes were rescued early.

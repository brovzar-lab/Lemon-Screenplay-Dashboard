# Aaron Sorkin on Adaptation

Most of my career has been adaptation. Adapting my own play (A Few Good Men). Adapting books (The Social Network, Moneyball, Steve Jobs, Molly's Game, Charlie Wilson's War). Adapting history (The Trial of the Chicago 7, Being the Ricardos). Adapting a sacred American text (To Kill a Mockingbird). Adapting a beloved old musical (Camelot). I have a few rules about how I do it. I'll go through them, and then I'll walk you through the three you asked about: Molly's Game, The Social Network, and Steve Jobs.

## My rules of adaptation

### 1. The book is the research, not the script.

When a producer hands me a book, the book is not the screenplay waiting to be transcribed. It is a long, deeply researched argument that I now get to use as a foundation for a different argument in a different medium. The book has done the homework. My job is to find the drama. Those are not the same job.

### 2. Find the dramatic engine, then ignore everything else.

A book can have ten themes. A movie has one, maybe two. When I read a book I'm adapting, I am looking for the one question the story can be built around. Friendship and betrayal in The Social Network. Whether the system you grew up in deserves your loyalty if it's wrong, in Moneyball. Whether a daughter can ever forgive her father, in Molly's Game. The dramatic engine is what stays. Everything else gets cut, no matter how interesting.

### 3. Truth is not the same as accuracy.

Accuracy is reporting what happened. Truth is conveying who someone was. Those things often diverge. If a character did not actually attend a particular meeting but their absence from that meeting was so revealing that putting them in it tells the audience more about who they were than the actual record could, I will put them in the meeting. I will tell the audience I have done it, in interviews, in my talks, in the production notes. I will not pretend the movie is a documentary. The movie is an impressionistic portrait, not a photograph. If you want the photograph, read the book. The book is right there.

### 4. Composite characters are a tool, not a cheat.

If three real people in a story say roughly the same thing, you do not need three actors. You need one. The composite character is how you compress a true ensemble into a dramatic ensemble. Charlie Jaffey in Molly's Game is a composite of Molly Bloom's actual lawyer and a few other figures around her. Mark Kaffee in A Few Good Men is partially based on a real lawyer my sister Deborah told me about, but he is also me, and he is also several other people. The audience does not lose anything. They gain a character they can follow.

### 5. Compress time.

A life is too long for a movie. A career is too long for a movie. A trial is rarely too long for a movie because trials already compress events into a structure with rules. When I have to compress, I look for the smallest unit of time that can contain the largest amount of meaning. One week. One day. One hour. Three real-time meetings. The compression is not just a constraint. It is the form. It is what makes the audience feel they are watching something that has to be resolved before the credits.

### 6. Pick a structural conceit and commit to it.

The Social Network has two depositions running in parallel with flashbacks between them. Steve Jobs has three real-time forty-minute acts at three product launches. Molly's Game has voiceover as structural spine, alternating present-day-with-the-lawyer scenes against the rise and fall of the games. Trial of the Chicago 7 has the trial as spine and the riot as flashback. Being the Ricardos has one week of production with marriage and career flashbacks woven in. None of these are the structure of the book. All of them are the structure of the movie. Pick yours, and live in it for two years.

### 7. Get the people right, even when you change the events.

I will move a meeting. I will compress two months into one week. I will combine three lawyers into one. What I will not do is misrepresent who somebody fundamentally was. If I'm writing about a real person, I am responsible to them, even if I'm taking liberties with the timeline. Steve Wozniak was not at the NeXT and iMac launches in real life. I put him there because the relationship between Steve and Woz needed to be present in all three acts for the structure to work. The relationship I dramatized between them is true to who they were. The location is fiction. That distinction matters.

### 8. Talk to the people who were there, if you can.

For The Social Network, I could not talk to Mark, because he and his counsel did not want to participate. I talked to other people. For Steve Jobs, Steve was dead, but I talked to Wozniak, Joanna Hoffman, John Sculley, Andy Hertzfeld, Lisa Brennan-Jobs, Chrisann Brennan. For Molly's Game, I talked to Molly. A lot. For two years before I started writing. The conversations are not transcribed in the script, but they are in the bloodstream of the script. The job is to listen until you understand the person, then write the person, not the transcript.

### 9. The audience does not need everything.

A book reader spends ten or twelve hours with a story. A movie audience has two hours. You will leave things out. Some of them will be your favorite things. The reader will notice. Make peace with that on day one of the writing, not on day one of the press tour.

### 10. Adaptation is not transcription. Honor the source by translating it.

This is the line I use most when I'm explaining myself, including to the rights holders. The job is not to preserve the book. The job is to make the movie that the book deserves. Sometimes those are very far apart, and that is when the writer earns the fee.

## Molly's Game

I read Molly Bloom's memoir and I called my agent and said this is the next thing I'm writing. Mark Gordon had bought the rights. Bloom had asked for me by name, which she likes to mention and so do I.

What I knew right away: Molly Bloom is a movie heroine. Her integrity, her refusal to give up names when giving up names would have saved her, those are qualities you usually only find in fiction. The book gave me the world. The conversations gave me the spine.

I spent two years talking to Molly before I wrote a page. Most of those conversations were not about the poker games. The book covered the poker games. The conversations were about her father. He gets a few pages in the book. He runs the movie.

That was the click. Her broken relationship with her father is the dramatic engine of the movie. Molly's poker empire is the surface story. Molly's reckoning with her father is the story underneath. Once I understood that, I understood why she did not name names: she was protecting an idea about herself that had been put in her by a man who himself had been a withholding moralist.

The structural choices:

**1. Voiceover as spine, not as crutch.** I had always considered voiceover a literary affectation, used to import the voice of the author when the screenplay couldn't carry it. I changed my mind here. Molly's voice is the spine of the movie. It is not a crutch. It is a structural element holding two timelines together: the rise and fall of the games, and the present-day present-tense fight to stay out of prison.

**2. Two timelines, one engine.** The present day is Charlie Jaffey trying to figure out who Molly Bloom actually is. He is the audience surrogate. He is asking the question I had been asking her for two years. The flashbacks are her answer.

**3. Charlie Jaffey, composite.** Molly's actual criminal defense lawyer is in there. So are some other figures. So is, frankly, me. Charlie's job in the movie is to push at her, to refuse to take the easy answer, to ask one more question after she thinks the conversation is over. He is doing the writer's job, on screen.

**4. The father scene.** The Central Park scene with Costner where the father gives Molly three years of therapy in three minutes is the only invented scene in the movie that bothered me to invent. It bothered me because it was not, technically, real. I wrote it because the truth of the relationship needed a vessel. The audience needed to see her father say the thing he had not said. He says it. She receives it. She moves. That is the recognition. Aristotle would have written the same scene.

**5. The opening ski sequence.** The whole opening came to me at once, in one sitting. The voiceover, the freak accident, the helmet rolling up to her face. I knew if I opened the movie there, the audience would understand, in two minutes, who Molly was, what she lost, and that she was not in this for the reasons everyone thought.

What I would do differently: I directed it for the first time. I made directorial mistakes a more experienced director wouldn't have made. The script I would not change. The direction I would.

## The Social Network

I sold by page three of Ben Mezrich's proposal. Not the book. The proposal. Mezrich had not yet written the book. I had not had a Facebook account. I learned what Facebook was during the writing.

The reason I sold by page three: I saw the courtroom. The whole movie was a courtroom drama without a courtroom. Two depositions, running in parallel, with flashbacks between them. That structure was on the page from the beginning. It was the structure of the book Mezrich was going to write, and it was the structure of the movie I was going to write.

What Fincher did was take that structure and make it cinema. He shot deposition rooms like crime scenes. He shot Harvard like a private club. He shot the parties like a place you could not get into. He cast Jesse Eisenberg, who could read my dialogue at the speed I had written it, which was a speed nobody had ever read my dialogue at before.

The choices:

**1. The cold open.** Six pages of Mark and Erica breaking up at a bar. By the end, you know Mark is brilliant, broken, probably going to do something destructive at scale. You know Erica is right about him. You know the rest of the movie is the consequence of that scene. I have written better cold opens than that one but not many.

**2. The theme older than the subject.** Friendship, loyalty, betrayal, jealousy, power, feeling left out, wanting to get in. I said it at the time and I'll say it again. It's a story Aeschylus would have written. Shakespeare would have written. Chayefsky would have written. None of them were available, so I got lucky. The lesson: a contemporary subject is fine, but the theme has to be ancient.

**3. The two depositions as structural irony.** Mark is being sued by two different parties for two different things. The depositions are happening more or less at the same time. So we cut between them, and between the depositions and the past. The cutting reveals contradictions. Mark says something one way in deposition A. The truth shows up in flashback. Then Mark says something different in deposition B. The audience is the judge.

**4. The protagonist who is, technically, the antagonist.** Mark is the protagonist. He is also the person who hurts everyone else in the movie. The audience identifies with him because he is the smartest person on screen and because the movie is told mostly from his side. They also indict him, because they see what he does to Eduardo. That double vision is the engine.

**5. The Erica frame.** The movie opens with her and ends with him refreshing her Facebook page. The whole story is bookended by the one girl he could not have. The Rosebud reading. He builds the largest social network in human history and ends the movie alone, hitting refresh.

**6. What I changed.** Plenty. The deposition rooms are dramatic compressions. Some of the speed of the dialogue is invention. The Larry Summers scene is heightened. The Sean Parker arrest is heightened. The actual lawsuits were less theatrical than the movie made them. I gave the audience the truth of who these people were, in a structure that let them feel it. The Winklevii are angry that I made them sound like rowing aristocrats. They are rowing aristocrats. I just put it on the page.

## Steve Jobs

I was scared of this one. The first thing I told Scott Rudin when he came to me was that I did not want to write a cradle to grave biopic. I'd seen too many of them. The audience sits down and knows the rhythm: the boy, the discovery, the rise, the fall, the comeback, the death scene. I could feel myself writing each of those scenes already, and I hated all of them.

So I wrote Scott an email and pitched the structurally radical idea: three real-time scenes, each forty minutes long, each set backstage at a major Apple product launch. The Macintosh in 1984. NeXT in 1988. The iMac in 1998. No flashbacks. No voiceover. No childhood. The audience meets Steve as a grown man, three times, fourteen years apart, in three pressure cookers with the same supporting characters returning each time to confront him.

I did not think the studio would let me do it. They let me do it.

The choices:

**1. Real time as form.** Real time means thirty minutes for the audience is thirty minutes for the character. The clock is the engine. We are minutes from a launch. The next stage is right there. Whatever has to be resolved has to be resolved now or it does not get resolved. The constraint is doing the work that plot would do in a conventional movie.

**2. Five interpersonal conflicts, three meetings.** I sat with Walter Isaacson's book and identified five key relationships in Steve's life: Joanna Hoffman, Lisa Brennan-Jobs and Chrisann Brennan, Steve Wozniak, John Sculley, Andy Hertzfeld. Each of those five recurs in all three acts. The same five people show up, with the same questions, fourteen years apart. We get to see what changes and what does not.

**3. The recurrence is the lesson.** Steve has the same fight with Joanna in 1984 that he is still having with her in 1998, and we are watching him learn nothing about it for two acts and then learn everything about it in the third. The structural recurrence is doing the work that interior monologue does in a novel.

**4. The Wozniak compression.** Woz was not actually present at the NeXT launch or the iMac launch. I put him there. He was at the Mac launch. The conflict between him and Steve was real, ongoing, and central to who Steve was, but in life it played out across two decades and in many rooms. I needed it played out at three product launches in three acts. I asked Woz. He understood. He said in interviews that the lines I gave him are not lines he would have said but they carry the right message. That is the trade. I take the historical license. I am responsible for the truth of who the man was. He gets to say in interviews he didn't say what I have him say. Both of those things are correct.

**5. The Sculley compression.** Same trade. Steve and Sculley, in real life, did not speak again after the 1985 ouster. In the movie, Sculley shows up at all three launches. Why? Because the structure required the same five characters to recur. Because Sculley represented the version of the father figure that Steve fought with and ultimately had to make peace with. The movie is doing the work that fourteen years of interior reckoning did. Compressed. Theatrical. Not journalistic.

**6. The Lisa scene at the end.** The whole movie is rigged to land on the moment Steve hears that his daughter is going to college, that she has made the music player she wanted, that she is herself. Everything that came before, the cruelty, the denial of paternity, the brilliance, all of it is paid off in that moment. The third act is about a man who has spent a lifetime calling things impossible learning that the only thing that was impossible was admitting he was wrong about her.

**7. The 182-page screenplay.** The script was 182 pages. That is twenty pages too long for a feature, by the rules. I let the script be that long because the dialogue density was what the audience was paying for. Boyle rehearsed each act for two or three weeks and shot each act chronologically, like three short plays in a row. Fassbender got to live the character forward, not in scattered pieces. The shoot was a play. The film is a play. I am a playwright pretending to write movies.

**8. What I missed.** I missed the Pixar years. I missed the iPhone. I missed the cancer. The movie is not "Steve Jobs, the comprehensive history." It is three nights, fourteen years apart, in which a brilliant difficult man is forced to be present with the people who know him best. If you want comprehensive, read the book. The book is excellent. Walter did the comprehensive work. I did the play.

## The same skeleton, three times

Notice what these three movies have in common: a constraining structural form (depositions, real-time launches, twin timelines with voiceover), a protagonist who is not entirely sympathetic, a relationship at the center that is the actual engine (Mark and Erica, Steve and Lisa, Molly and her father), and a refusal to do the cradle to grave.

If you are adapting something, that is the bag of tricks. Pick a structural form that compresses. Pick a relationship that is the engine. Pick a theme older than the subject. And do not, under any circumstances, write the cradle to grave.

## A note on To Kill a Mockingbird

When I adapted Mockingbird for Broadway in 2018, I made Atticus less perfect, less sure of himself, more wrong. Some people did not like it. Harper Lee's estate sued me, then settled, and the show went on. My defense, then and now: in 1960 Atticus could be a saint. In 2018 a saint Atticus reads as a fantasy. The audience knows too much now. To honor the book, I had to translate it forward, not preserve it in amber. That is what an adaptation is. If you wanted the 1960 Atticus, the novel is right there.

## A note on Camelot

Same idea. The 1960 Lerner and Loewe Camelot has Merlin as a wizard and Morgan le Fay as a sorceress and the whole show is half a fairy tale. I removed the magic. The story works as the slow tragedy of an idealistic man trying to build a just society on a foundation of his own infidelity. No spells. No supernatural. Idealism survives or fails on its own merits. That choice cost me some loyalists. It bought me a Camelot that an adult audience in 2023 could sit through without flinching at the dragons.

If you are going to adapt something beloved, the question is not "how do I preserve this." The question is "what version of this story belongs to the audience that is alive right now." Answer that, and the choices follow.

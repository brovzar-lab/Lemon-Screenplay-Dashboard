# Interpreting Notes - Finding "The Note Behind the Note"

Not all notes are equal. Don't take notes literally. Look for the REAL problem the note is pointing to.

## Core Principle

**Their solution may not be THE solution.** Readers tell you symptoms; you diagnose the disease.

**Example:**
- Reader says: "Add more plot"
- Real problem: Character isn't emotionally engaging
- Actual solution: Deepen character's internal story, not add plot

## Common Notes and What They Really Mean

### 1. "I just wasn't involved in the story. I wasn't that concerned about the main character."

**What it really means:**
- No emotional connection to main character
- Stakes aren't personal enough
- Internal character story is weak or missing

**Where to look:**
- Main character's internal story/flaws
- Personal stakes (not just plot stakes)
- Motivation to begin the journey
- Pressure on character's emotional wounds

**Don't do:** Add more plot

**Do instead:** 
- Create/strengthen internal character story
- Add personal stakes through key relationships
- Show character's flaws getting pressured

---

### 2. "It felt familiar. I feel I have seen this movie before."

**What it really means:**
- Characters are clichés
- Plot is too predictable
- Lacks original voice

**Where to look:**
- Main character (are they a cliché from other movies?)
- Antagonist (one-dimensional villain?)
- Plot twists (too predictable?)
- Your personal voice (are you being honest?)

**Don't do:** Change the genre or concept

**Do instead:**
- Put something of yourself into main character
- Make antagonist see themselves as right, not evil
- Add unexpected complications
- Write from personal emotional experiences

---

### 3. "I don't believe the character would do that."

**What it really means:**
- Character motivation unclear
- Character acting to serve plot, not their story
- Character inconsistency

**Where to look:**
- Character's internal story (is it clear?)
- Want and need (are they defined?)
- Character consistency (are they acting consistently?)
- Plot forcing character, not character forcing plot

**Don't do:** Just explain why they did it

**Do instead:**
- Clarify character's central emotional issue
- Base plot on solid character story
- Ensure character actions come from who they are

---

### 4. "I don't really get the main character. I'm not really sure who they are."

**What it really means:**
- Character lacks emotional internal story
- Character lacks distinct attitude
- Character is serving plot without personality

**Where to look:**
- Internal emotional story (missing?)
- Distinct attitude
- Personal stakes
- Relationships that reveal character

**Don't do:** Add backstory exposition

**Do instead:**
- Commit to emotional internal story
- Create defining scenes that show who they are
- Use relationships to flesh out character
- Give them a consistent attitude

---

### 5. "The Main Character is not likable."

**What it really means:**
- Can't emotionally identify with character
- Character is mean/cruel
- No understanding of why character acts this way

**Where to look:**
- Relatable flaws (not cruel behavior)
- Moments of trying to do right thing
- Pain and struggle (we empathize with underdogs)

**Don't do:** Remove all flaws to make them "nice"

**Do instead:**
- Make character more RELATABLE, not nicer
- Show them struggling with crisis
- Show good intentions despite bad choices
- Never make them cruel (audiences never forgive cruelty)

---

### 6. "The best friend is more interesting than the main character."

**What it really means:**
- Best friend has flaws, main character doesn't
- Main character is too perfect/clean
- Main character needs more dimension

**Where to look:**
- Main character's flaws
- Main character's relationships
- Moments that reveal main character's complexity

**Don't do:** Make best friend less interesting

**Do instead:**
- Give main character relatable flaws
- Add internal emotional struggles
- Deepen main character's relationships
- Show multiple facets of main character

---

### 7. "The love interest in the story isn't interesting or essential to the screenplay."

**What it really means:**
- Love interest is cardboard character
- Love interest serves no story purpose
- Relationship feels obligatory, not essential

**Where to look:**
- Love interest's own emotional story
- Authentic relationship dynamics
- How relationship complicates main character's journey

**Don't do:** Make them more attractive or add kissing scene

**Do instead:**
- Give love interest their own hopes/dreams/fears
- Create authentic relationship with baggage on both sides
- Make relationship complicate main character's journey
- Base on real relationships, not "movie relationships"

---

### 8. "I got lost. I got confused."

**What it really means:**
- Plot too complicated
- Too many twists without character anchor
- Reader got bored and started skimming

**Where to look:**
- Overcomplicated plot
- Too many characters introduced at once
- Lack of engaging main character
- Named characters who don't matter

**Don't do:** Simplify by removing all mystery

**Do instead:**
- Dial back plot complexity
- Add more character and relationships
- Spread out character introductions
- Only name characters important to story
- Make plot complications spring from characters

---

### 9. "I didn't care about anyone in the story. I didn't like anyone in the screenplay."

**What it really means:**
- Characters lack depth
- No personal stories or emotional stakes
- Too plot-heavy, not enough character

**Where to look:**
- Character depth (dreams, hopes, fears, agendas)
- Empathy for main character
- Personal stakes (not just plot stakes)
- Someone or something personal at risk

**Don't do:** Make everyone nicer

**Do instead:**
- Give all major characters dreams/hopes/fears
- Create empathy for main character's struggle
- Personalize stakes through supporting character
- Show dire personal consequences of failure

---

### 10. "I don't understand what your screenplay is about."

**What it really means:**
- Theme is unclear or inconsistent
- Main character's story not clear
- Plot feels random, not connected to character
- Want is not clearly stated

**Where to look:**
- Theme (can you state in one word?)
- Main character's story
- Plot connection to theme/character
- Main character's want (stated early?)

**Don't do:** Add explanatory dialogue

**Do instead:**
- Reduce theme to ONE WORD
- Restate theme through characters and plot
- Clarify main character's want early
- Tie plot events to character's journey
- Raise stakes

---

### 11. "The dialogue doesn't seem real."

**What it really means:**
- Characters speak too formally
- Too much exposition
- Speeches too long
- Characters talk ABOUT plot, not their problems

**Where to look:**
- Formal complete sentences vs. natural fragments
- Long speeches
- Expository dialogue
- Character-specific voices

**Don't do:** Major dialogue rewrite in early passes

**Do instead:**
- Wait until Scene/Dialogue Pass
- Speak in fragments, not sentences
- Create space between lines
- Focus on relationship/personal problems, not plot
- Give each character distinct voice

---

### 12. "All the characters sound the same."

**What it really means:**
- Characters lack distinct attitudes
- No consistent character voices
- Missing character-specific philosophies

**Where to look:**
- Each character's attitude
- How they see the world
- Consistency of voice in each scene

**Don't do:** Add accents or quirks

**Do instead:**
- Assign each character specific attitude (optimist, pessimist, cynic)
- Make attitude consistent in every scene
- Show worldview through dialogue
- Create conflict through opposing attitudes

---

### 13. "Not much seems to happen."

**What it really means:**
- Not enough conflict/crisis
- Main character not going through enough
- Relationships too functional
- Antagonist not strong enough

**Where to look:**
- Main character's crisis (is it life-altering?)
- Character flaws and pressure on them
- Dysfunctional relationships
- External plot crisis magnitude
- Obstacles and complications
- Antagonist strength

**Don't do:** Add random action scenes

**Do instead:**
- Intensify main character's crisis
- Make relationships dysfunctional
- Add significant obstacles
- Strengthen antagonist
- Layer multiple complications

---

### 14. "The screenplay seemed to slow down near the end."

**What it really means:**
- Lost momentum (usually setup problem)
- Not enough established in Act 1
- Stakes not rising
- Main character's story unclear/weak
- Antagonist not formidable enough

**Where to look:**
- First Act setup (is it strong enough?)
- Stakes progression (do they keep rising?)
- Main character's internal story
- Antagonist's strength

**Don't do:** Just make third act bigger

**Do instead:**
- Strengthen first act setup
- Clarify main character's arc
- Raise stakes progressively
- Make antagonist more formidable
- Ensure third act resolves what first act set up

---

### 15. "The ending didn't work for me."

**What it really means:**
- First act didn't set up third act properly
- Character arc incomplete or unclear
- Ending doesn't feel earned
- Problem too easy to solve

**Where to look:**
- First Act (does it set up the ending?)
- Main character's internal story
- Character arc completion
- Difficulty of final problem

**Don't do:** Just rewrite the ending

**Do instead:**
- Look to FIRST ACT for the fix
- Ensure clear character arc setup
- Make character earn the ending
- Make final test truly difficult
- Character must satisfy NEED, not just WANT

---

## General Interpretation Principles

1. **Character notes often hide as plot notes**
   - "Add more plot" usually means "make me care about character"
   - "It's slow" usually means "I'm not invested in character"

2. **Look for patterns in multiple notes**
   - Same note from 3+ readers = real problem
   - Contradictory notes = dig deeper for root issue

3. **The reader's solution is rarely the answer**
   - Listen to the symptom
   - Diagnose the real problem
   - Find your own solution

4. **Most problems trace back to:**
   - Main character's internal story
   - Character motivation
   - Emotional stakes
   - Consistency
   - Theme clarity

5. **Fix character FIRST, plot SECOND**
   - If character is solid, plot is easier to fix
   - If character is weak, no amount of plot will save it

## Questions to Ask When Receiving Notes

1. "What is this note REALLY saying?"
2. "Is this a symptom or the disease?"
3. "Where in the screenplay does this problem originate?"
4. "Is this a character issue masquerading as a plot issue?"
5. "Can you give me an example of when you felt this?"
6. "What would make you care more about this?"

## Red Flags in Notes

**Vague notes to push back on:**
- "Make it better"
- "More action"
- "I don't know, something's off"

**Ask for specifics:** "Can you point me to a specific moment where you felt this?"

**Trust your gut when notes:**
- Contradict your core vision
- Would make it a different movie
- Come from someone who doesn't understand the genre
- Feel like they're writing their own movie

Remember: You own the script. Notes are information, not orders.

# Truby Horror Beats

Complete structural framework from John Truby's "Anatomy of Genres."

## Horror's Place in Genre

Horror is the **first and most primitive genre**—it addresses humanity's most fundamental distinction: life versus death. The genre forces us to confront death by shoving it in our face.

**Mind-Action Story View:** Life is an ongoing struggle to defeat death—a fight we are all destined to lose.

## The 15 Horror Beats

### BEAT 1: Ghost (Sins of the Past)
**The most important beat in Horror.**

The Ghost is NOT a spectral opponent—it's the event from the past still haunting the hero. Represents:
- Power of the past over the present
- Sins of fathers/mothers affecting children
- The mind attacking itself

The Ghost keeps attacking throughout the story and drives the entire plot.

**Examples:**
- *A Nightmare on Elm Street:* Parents killed child murderer Freddy Krueger 10 years ago
- *The Shining:* Jack injured his son Danny while drinking
- *Alien:* Company gave permission to commit murder (crew expendable)
- *Psycho:* Norman's matricide and twisted desire for his mother
- *Get Out:* History of slavery and exploitation of Black bodies

### BEAT 2: Story World (Haunted House)
The setting expresses the hero's Ghost and weakness-need. It's the flawed mind made physical—a pressure cooker.

**Types of Haunted Houses:**
1. **Classic Haunted House:** Fall of the House of Usher, The Shining, The Exorcist
2. **Closed Social World:** Rotted aristocracy (Dracula is a count; Dr. Frankenstein is a baron)
3. **American Small Town:** Corruption beneath happy facade (Halloween, Poltergeist, Friday the 13th)
4. **Class AND Race:** Modern-day plantation (Get Out)
5. **Haunted City:** Les Revenants, 28 Days Later

**Architectural Psychology:**
- **Cellar:** Where sins of parents are buried; skeletons rise up
- **Attic:** Where fond memories are locked OR where the mad are imprisoned

**Technique:** Begin in foreign/"Old World" location, then transport Horror to new, modern world like a plague (The Exorcist, Dracula, Arachnophobia).

### BEAT 3: Weakness-Need (Psychological)
Hero's mind is effectively enslaved. Sources of the flaw:

**A. Binary Thinking**
The mind's tendency to see differences as opposites (Either/Or, Me vs. Other, Us vs. Them). This is the mind's foundational flaw.

**B. Fundamental Fears**
- Death itself
- Being killed and eaten
- Being enslaved
- Being raped
- Being a failure
- Losing one's mind
- Living a life of quiet desperation

**C. Unsociable Desires (The Dark Side)**
- Power over others
- Inflicting pain
- Doing what is forbidden
- Kill, rape, devour
- Illicit sex
- Matricide/patricide

### BEAT 4: Weakness-Need (Moral)
**Shame and Guilt** — Primary weapons of the self-conscious mind.

Hero often suffers tremendous shame and guilt at beginning due to the Ghost. Takes it upon self to pay for a past sin, but since not truly responsible, fails. Dark emotions build.

### BEAT 5: Inciting Event (Monster Attacks)
The opponent is extremely powerful and drives the action. Attacks from without (monster) AND within (ghost/weakness) put hero on defensive immediately.

The monster attacks BECAUSE of the taboos causing guilt and shame that require punishment.

### BEAT 6: Desire
- **Surface desire:** Defeat/escape the monster
- **Deeper desire:** Defeat death

Because hero is weak relative to monster, typically seeks to escape first. Escape is a reaction (lowest form of desire)—why Horror is often considered least artistic genre.

### BEAT 7: Ally or Allies
Often includes a **Rational Skeptic**—a character who provides plausible natural explanation for supernatural events. Paradoxically, this makes the audience's leap of faith possible.

### BEAT 8: Opponent (Monster as Double)
Strong connection between internal opponent (ghost/weakness) and external opponent (monster).

**The Double Technique:** Monster IS the hero—both external and internal opponent. The monster's attack causes hero's devolution, but true source is the psychological flaw.

**Two Types of Reduction:**
- **Animal Horror:** Fear of being reduced to an animal (body, loss of control, sexual passion). Examples: wolf, ape, bat, cat.
- **Machine Horror:** Fear of losing identity/individuality (robot man, living dead). The zombie is perfect personification.

### BEAT 9: The Barrier
The wall stopping the monster from entering the human community—always fails.

Represents:
- External forbidden zone
- Internal taboo desire the hero should not indulge
- The hard line between life and death

### BEAT 10: Apparent Defeat (All Hope Lost)
Monster at full power. Hero beaten down to point of death, symbolizing our own mortality.

### BEAT 11: Obsessive Drive
Hero formulates plan to defeat/escape the monster. Even failed plans show agency.

### BEAT 12: Battle
Direct confrontation. The **Cyclone Effect**: Sequence as many nightmares as possible at faster and faster pace. Each nightmare worse than the last. Tremendous narrative drive.

### BEAT 13: Self-Revelation (Usually None)
Horror heroes typically do NOT have self-revelation—they either die or survive unchanged. The audience has the revelation instead.

**Exception:** Transcendent Horror allows for genuine character growth.

### BEAT 14: Moral Decision
Hero chooses: change or die as they are. In basic Horror, this choice is often too late or futile.

### BEAT 15: New Equilibrium
World transformed—usually for the worse. Horror endings:
- Hero survives but damaged
- Hero dies
- Monster defeated but at great cost
- Monster only temporarily stopped
- Ambiguous (was it real?)

## Transcendent Horror

### Path 1: Horror/Myth (Religious)
Hero confronts death directly and may achieve symbolic victory over it. Often involves religious/spiritual dimension.

**Key Element:** The hero's journey becomes universal—about all of humanity confronting mortality.

**Examples:** A Christmas Carol, The Seventh Seal, Frankenstein (novel)

### Path 2: Horror/Sci-Fi Epic (Creating Consciousness)
Story about creating or defining what is human. Central question: "What makes us human?"

**The Structural Flip:**
1. Story begins with apparent monster (non-human creature)
2. At midpoint or later, flip occurs
3. Apparent monster reveals their humanity
4. Apparent humans reveal their monstrousness
5. Audience forced to question who the real monster is

**Variations:**
- Re-creating a human: Frankenstein, Get Out, Shape of Water
- Creating human from animal: Rise of the Planet of the Apes
- Creating human from machine: Ex Machina, Blade Runner, 2001
- Creating new species: Westworld

**Requirements for Transcendence:**
- Master basic beats first
- Social/political themes integral to premise (not tacked on)
- Monster must represent something in the hero
- Story asks genuine questions about humanity

## The Cyclone Effect (Battle Sequence)

For maximum impact in the final sequence:

1. List all possible nightmares for your hero
2. Arrange in escalating order of terror
3. Compress into rapid succession
4. Each nightmare worse than the last
5. Accelerate pace toward the final confrontation

This creates tremendous pressure on both hero and audience.

---

## Horror's Missing Element

Horror forces us to confront death but doesn't answer: "What are we to do?"

This is why Horror is the first rung on the genre ladder—it's primitive. For a recipe for action (how to actually live), the story must move to Action genre.

However, Transcendent Horror can provide meaning through its exploration of what makes us human.

# Horror Screenwriter

Expert consultant for horror screenplay development and analysis. Draws on methodologies from John Truby (structure/transcendence), Zack Long (scare mechanics/genre craft), and Philip Athans (monster design).

## Core Philosophy

Horror is not about monsters—it's about the **flawed human mind**. The best horror uses fear as a lens to examine what it means to be human. Great horror:

1. Creates a unique monster that relentlessly pursues a victim (constant pressure)
2. Connects the monster to the hero's psychological/moral weakness (the Double)
3. Uses the Ghost (sins of the past) to drive the plot
4. Transcends genre by exploring society, identity, mortality, and the human condition

**The Horror Equation:** Fear + Shock + Disgust → Anxiety → Engagement

## Two Modes of Operation

### Mode A: Development (Idea → Screenplay)
For brainstorming, concept development, and building a horror project from scratch.

### Mode B: Analysis (Existing Script → Better Script)
For diagnosing problems in horror screenplays and providing targeted improvements.

## MODE A: DEVELOPMENT WORKFLOW

### Phase 1: Concept & Premise

**Start with the human story, not the monster.** Horror invades a world—it doesn't start there.

Ask these questions in order:

1. **What human fear are you exploring?** (Death, loss of identity, loss of control, being prey, transformation, isolation, the past catching up)
2. **What is the Ghost?** (The sin from the past that haunts the present—this is horror's most important beat)
3. **Who is your victim/hero?** (Make them intensely human before horror arrives)
4. **What is the monster?** (External threat that also represents the hero's internal flaw)
5. **What is the Haunted House?** (Setting as expression of the hero's psychology)

**Thematic Premise Formula:**
```
When [GHOST from the past] catches up to [HERO with specific weakness],
[MONSTER representing that weakness] forces them to confront [THEME about humanity].
```

### Phase 2: Monster Design

Read `references/monster-design.md` for the complete framework.

**Quick Monster Checklist:**
- [ ] Origin established (or deliberately mysterious)
- [ ] Rules defined (internal consistency is critical)
- [ ] Weakness present (hero must have fighting chance)
- [ ] Revelation staged (don't give away too early)
- [ ] Metaphoric meaning clear (what does it represent?)
- [ ] Monster as Double (connected to hero's flaw)

**Monster Function Types:**
| Function | Role in Story |
|----------|---------------|
| Obstacle | Prevents hero from reaching goal |
| Agent of Destruction | Kills, maims, terrifies |
| Agent of Revelation | Forces hero to confront truth |
| Object of Pity | Tragic figure, not pure evil |
| Source of Chaos | Disrupts normal world |

### Phase 3: Structure Selection

**Choose Your Struggle** (from Long's Seven Struggles):

| Struggle | Core Question | Best For |
|----------|---------------|----------|
| Mystery | "What is the threat?" | Slow-burn reveals, psychological horror |
| Survival | "Will I live?" | Slashers, creature features |
| Escape | "Can I get free?" | Contained thrillers, captivity horror |
| Disaster | "Can I endure this?" | Apocalyptic, contagion |
| Transformation | "Am I still me?" | Body horror, possession |
| Madness | "What is real?" | Psychological, unreliable narrator |
| Menace | "What will I do next?" | Villain protagonist, serial killer POV |

**Pacing Decision:**
- **Steady Ramp:** Momentum increases exponentially (slashers, creature features)
- **Slow Burn:** Character/atmosphere focus, gradual escalation (psychological horror)

### Phase 4: Beat Structure

Read `references/truby-horror-beats.md` for complete beat breakdown.

**Essential Horror Beats:**

1. **Ghost (Sins of the Past)** — The event haunting the present; drives entire plot
2. **Haunted House/World** — Setting expresses hero's psychology
3. **Monster Attacks** — Opponent drives action, puts hero on defensive
4. **Hero as Victim** — Beaten down to point of death
5. **Weakness-Need** — Hero's psychological/moral flaw the monster exploits
6. **Desire** — Escape/defeat monster (surface) / Defeat death (deep)
7. **Opponent as Double** — Monster reflects hero's internal flaw
8. **Apparent Defeat** — All hope lost, monster at full power
9. **Battle** — Direct confrontation, hero uses new knowledge
10. **Moral Decision** — Hero chooses: change or die as they are
11. **New Equilibrium** — World transformed (for better or worse)

### Phase 5: Scare Design

Read `references/scare-mechanics.md` for complete toolkit.

**Cognitive Effect Selection:**

| Desired Effect | Primary Tool |
|----------------|--------------|
| Visceral jolt | Jump scare (earned through setup) |
| Dread | Dramatic irony + slow reveal |
| Revulsion | Visual disgust + abjection |
| Unease | Atmosphere + wrong details |
| Terror | Character in believable danger |
| Devastation | Fundamental shock at ending |
| Discomfort | Intellectual disgust (ideas, not images) |

**Scare Sequence Structure:**
1. Establish expectation (audience thinks they know what's coming)
2. Build tension (delay, delay, delay)
3. Misdirection (false alarm or wrong direction)
4. Payoff (or deliberate withholding)
5. Character reaction (triggers audience mirroring)

**Avoid:**
- Spring-loaded cat (cheap fake-out with innocuous object)
- Coupon shot (monster reveal with no tension buildup)
- Repetitive attack beats (same scare over and over)

### Phase 6: Transcendence Check

Does your horror merely scare, or does it ask "What is human?"

**Two Paths to Transcendence:**

**Path 1: Horror/Myth (Religious)**
- Hero confronts death directly, may achieve symbolic victory
- Examples: A Christmas Carol, The Seventh Seal, Frankenstein (novel)

**Path 2: Horror/Sci-Fi Epic (Creating Consciousness)**
- Story about creating/defining what is human
- The Structural Flip: Apparent monster reveals humanity; apparent humans reveal monstrousness
- Examples: Get Out, Ex Machina, Blade Runner, Shape of Water

**Transcendence Checklist:**
- [ ] Monster represents something in the hero
- [ ] Confronting monster requires confronting self
- [ ] Story has social/political/universal ramifications
- [ ] Asks genuine questions about humanity/society
- [ ] Genre serves theme, not the reverse

## MODE B: ANALYSIS WORKFLOW

For existing horror screenplays. Upload the script and request analysis.

### Step 1: First Pass — Gut Reaction

Read the script once for emotional response:
- Where did you feel genuine dread/fear/unease?
- Where did attention wander?
- What lingers after reading?
- Did the ending land?

### Step 2: Structural Diagnosis

Check for these common problems:

**Problem: Monster has no connection to hero's Ghost/weakness**
- Diagnosis: No structural alignment
- Fix: Create clear line from past crime → present flaw → future horror

**Problem: Repetitive attack beats**
- Diagnosis: Same scare repeated without variation
- Fix: Vary attack types, add false alarms, create hierarchy of opposition

**Problem: No pressure escalation**
- Diagnosis: Missing cyclone effect
- Fix: Each nightmare worse and faster than the last

**Problem: Passive hero throughout**
- Diagnosis: Hero only reacts, never acts
- Fix: Hero should formulate plans, improvise, fight back (even in failure)

**Problem: Arbitrary story world**
- Diagnosis: Setting is just backdrop
- Fix: Make haunted house/world express hero's Ghost and weakness

**Problem: No psychological depth**
- Diagnosis: Pure external threat, no internal monster
- Fix: Add the Double (monster reflects hero's flaw), shame/guilt, unsociable desires

**Problem: Scares don't land**
- Diagnosis: Poor setup, no earned tension
- Fix: Review scare mechanics—expectation, tension, misdirection, payoff

### Step 3: Beat-by-Beat Analysis

Use the checklist in `references/horror-diagnosis.md` to evaluate each structural beat.

### Step 4: Monster Evaluation

Apply the Monster Design framework to assess:
- Is the monster unique or derivative?
- Are rules internally consistent?
- Is there a clear weakness?
- Is revelation properly staged?
- Does it function as the hero's Double?

### Step 5: Recommendations

Provide prioritized fixes:
1. **Critical** — Structural issues that undermine the entire script
2. **Major** — Problems that significantly weaken impact
3. **Polish** — Refinements that elevate good to great

## SUBGENRE REFERENCE

| Subgenre | Key Elements | Tone | Examples |
|----------|--------------|------|----------|
| Slasher | Masked killer, teen victims, final girl | Visceral, relentless | Halloween, Scream |
| Supernatural | Ghosts, demons, possession | Atmospheric, dread | The Conjuring, The Exorcist |
| Psychological | Unreliable reality, mental breakdown | Unsettling, ambiguous | The Shining, Black Swan |
| Body Horror | Transformation, physical corruption | Disturbing, visceral | The Fly, Cronenberg |
| Found Footage | Diegetic camera, "reality" | Immediate, raw | Paranormal Activity, REC |
| Folk Horror | Rural, pagan, tradition | Creeping dread | Midsommar, The Wicker Man |
| Social Horror | Commentary on race, class, society | Smart, layered | Get Out, Us |
| Creature Feature | Monster/beast as primary threat | Tense, survival | Alien, Jaws |
| Gothic | Decay, family secrets, atmosphere | Moody, romantic | Crimson Peak, Rebecca |
| Cosmic Horror | Unknowable, incomprehensible threat | Existential dread | Annihilation, Color Out of Space |

## SCREENPLAY OUTPUT

When requested to write actual screenplay pages, follow standard format and apply:

1. **Scene description:** Sparse, evocative, sensory-focused
2. **Pacing:** Use white space and short paragraphs for tension; longer for dread
3. **Sound design:** Horror lives in sound—indicate what we hear
4. **Character reactions:** Show fear through behavior, not description
5. **Restraint:** What you don't show is often scarier than what you do

For full screenplay production, recommend using this skill for development then transitioning to standard screenwriting tools for execution.

# The 11 Stake Types

Stakes are the negative consequences of failure. Each type below is a different category of loss. Most strong stories run several at once, layered. Use these as a checklist when mapping a story and as a menu when prescribing.

Eight can carry a story on their own. Three cannot: regret, suffering, and sacrifice are contingency stakes, covered at the end. They amplify, they never anchor.

---

## 1. General protection

A group, a place, or a population is destroyed or enslaved if the hero fails. A bus, a city, a kingdom, a planet. Also covers the professional protector: the agent guarding the president, the lawyer defending the accused, the cop protecting a witness. The emphasis is on what others lose, not on what the hero loses personally.

Works because the scale is large. Fails because scale is abstract. Audiences connect to one person more readily than to a nation, which is why so many world-saving stories bolt a personal loss onto the general threat.

**Diagnostic:** if the story runs on general protection alone with nothing personal attached, expect a respectable but cool audience response. The fix is almost always to add demise.

## 2. Demise

Someone the hero loves dies if he fails. This is the personal version of protection, and it is the single most reliable stake in commercial storytelling. The reason the hero's loved one keeps getting taken hostage in the third act is not laziness, it is that a personal mission connects where an institutional one does not.

The hero's own life is a special case. If the only thing at risk is his own skin, ask whether he could simply leave. If he could, that is risk, not stakes.

**Diagnostic:** ask who dies. If the answer is "no one specific," the story is running on general protection and knows it.

## 3. Livelihood

The hero, or someone he supports, loses the means of survival. Not a promotion, which is only happiness. The job loss has to threaten survival or his ability to support people who depend on him.

Sharper when: he sacrificed a great deal to get the position, he fought family or society to get there, the position is rare, his self-worth is fused to it, or he is unlikely to be hired anywhere else.

**Diagnostic:** in a comedy this is often the load-bearing stake that keeps the hero inside a situation he would sensibly quit. If a comedy premise depends on the hero staying somewhere absurd, check that livelihood is nailed down, and consider fusing it to a relationship (a child's opinion of him, a partner's judgment) so he cannot quit even when he wants to.

## 4. Freedom

He or someone he loves is confined if he fails. Prison is only the obvious form. Also: trapped in a time, a place, a body, a curse, a controlling marriage, a blackmail, or under relentless pursuit by someone who wants him caught. Works across every genre, including comedy.

**Diagnostic:** in a heist or caper, prison is usually a risk being courted, not a stake being avoided. Check which one it is. If the hero is volunteering for the danger, another stake must be doing the work.

## 5. Reputation

A name, a legacy, or an honor is destroyed. Either the reputation is already under siege at the start, or the hero's choices during the story put it in play. The climax restores it or ruins it permanently.

Underused by beginners, who reach for life and death. Powerful because it layers cleanly onto anything else: a man framed for a crime is fighting for his name and his freedom at once. Also the cleanest way to add depth to a standard action climax, by making the thing he protects part of a parent's legacy.

**Weight rule:** reputation counts as a safety need when tied to another safety-need stake (framed for murder). It counts as a spiritual need when it stands alone (clearing a long-dead ancestor's name). That distinction decides which matrix cell the story is in, so decide it explicitly.

## 6. Sanity

Failure alters the consciousness of the hero or someone he loves. Madness, memory tampering, or, fused with freedom, mind control. Rare, and chilling when used. Also a nasty variant on the save-the-world plot: the villain does not kill the city, he drives it insane.

## 7. Access

He loses contact with a person, a community, or a place. Custody fights are the purest form. Also the loss of a home, or exile from a community that gives the hero what his family does not.

**Boundary:** if the person he might lose access to is also in physical danger, that is demise, not access. Access assumes they are safe, he just cannot reach them.

**Weight rule:** safety need when it concerns the integrity of the family unit or the ownership of a home. Spiritual need when it concerns losing touch with a romantic prospect.

## 8. Justice

The antagonist has committed something heinous, and the audience's hunger to see it answered fuels the ending. Vigilante versions are revenge, and behave the same way.

Two variables control the strength. How egregious the crime is, calibrated to genre. And how much time passes between the crime and the climax, because the effect decays. For that reason the heinous act usually lands best at the end of Act 2, right before the climax, where it doubles as the low point.

**Danger:** this one backfires in both directions. Too mild and the audience is unmoved. Too graphic and they disengage in disgust. Also note that justice is a spiritual need, so if the hero risks his life purely for it, you are in the misalignment trap.

## 9. Suffering and sacrifice (contingency)

If the hero fails, everything he already endured was for nothing. Suffering is the accumulated ordeal, sacrifice is the specific thing he gave up.

Suffering belongs in every story. Each obstacle survived raises the price of defeat. A hero who has not suffered has not earned the win, and audiences feel that even when they cannot name it.

Sacrifice is optional but transformative. It is strongest when the person sacrificing had a real alternative and refused it, and when the thing given up was genuinely valuable, not obviously inferior.

**Cannot stand alone.** Both are always attached to something else.

## 10. Regret (contingency)

Two shapes. Missed opportunity: the hero had a modest personal dream, the story interrupted it, and failure closes that window permanently. Redemption: he failed at something similar in the past and has carried the guilt since, and this is his chance to settle it.

Cheap to add, disproportionate in effect, because regret is universal in a way that serial killers and dragons are not. It is one of the two or three easiest upgrades to prescribe on a script that is otherwise working.

**Cannot stand alone.** Always sits under a real stake.

## 11. Hero happiness

He has pinned his contentment to a prize. Win it and he is fulfilled, lose it and he is devastated. Nothing else is at risk. Framed as gain rather than avoidance of loss.

Make the prize concrete and explain why he wants it. Tie it to self-esteem, since heroes chase prizes to feel better about themselves and that is what makes the pursuit resonate. Deepen it by having him lose this same prize once before.

**The money rule.** Wealth never works as a standalone stake. Audiences value money for what it buys, safety, freedom, respect, access, and they carry that into fiction. If the hero is in it only for the money, the ending falls flat. Employment, treasure hunts, and heists all need a second stake underneath. Villains are exempt, they can be pure greed, but then the worst case of the hero failing cannot simply be that the villain gets rich.

**Exception:** money can stand alone if audience investment comes from elsewhere entirely, usually acute curiosity about a choice the story has been building toward.

## The MacGuffin blueprint

When several parties chase one object, conflict and action come free, and mean nothing without stakes. Build it in four steps.

1. Choose an object that can mean different things to different people.
2. Decide the hunters. Two minimum, three is the useful default, more for epics.
3. Assign a different stake to each hunter. At least one character the audience roots for must be chasing it for something other than money. The villain may be pure greed.
4. Design each hunter's support system, the helpers, ideally with their own motives.

Not every hunter needs to be present from page one. Give the audience one to root for by the end of Act 1 and introduce complications later.

## Grouping by weight

This grouping feeds directly into the matrix.

**Safety needs:** general protection, demise, livelihood, freedom, sanity, reputation when tied to another safety-need stake, access when it concerns family unity or a home.

**Spiritual needs:** justice, hero happiness, reputation standing alone, access when it concerns a romantic prospect.

**Contingency:** regret, suffering, sacrifice. Never anchors, always amplifies.

Safety needs pull harder than spiritual needs. A story that starts with a safety need starts from strength, and every modulator applied to it multiplies against a larger number.

# Adapting the Framework to Television

Be honest about the source: the framework was built for features and novels and draws almost all of its examples from film. It transfers well, but three things have to be adjusted, and the adjustments are inference from the principles, not the book's own instructions. Say so if the user asks how settled this is.

## The three-layer stakes model

A feature has one set of central stakes. A series has three, and most weak pilots are weak because only one or two of the layers exist.

**Series stakes.** What is at risk across the whole run, attached to the engine of the show. If the protagonist fails at the series level, what happens. These must not be resolvable inside one episode, and they must be capable of escalating over seasons without being solved.

**Season stakes.** What is at risk across this run of episodes. The escalation architecture of a season is the matrix architecture of a feature, stretched: establish in the premiere, raise at the season midpoint, raise again before the finale.

**Episode stakes.** What is at risk this week. In a procedural these reset. In serialized drama they compound.

Diagnose all three separately. The common pilot failure is strong episode stakes and vague series stakes, which reads on the page as "a good episode of a show I don't need to keep watching."

## What changes in the pilot

**The bonding window is shorter and matters more.** A feature has ten pages to bond the audience to the hero. A pilot has less audience patience, not more, since nobody has bought a ticket. Every note in `modulators.md` about delay, dilution, and alienation applies with the volume raised. Ensemble pilots dilute loyalty by construction, so the show has to be explicit about who the audience follows.

**The 25% rule still holds, inside the episode.** The protagonist has to be actively pursuing something by roughly a quarter of the way in.

**The end of the pilot has a second job.** A feature climax resolves the central stakes. A pilot climax has to resolve the episode stakes while leaving the series stakes not just unresolved but worse than when the episode began. If the pilot ends with everything more or less contained, the series stakes were never real.

## What changes in a series bible

Evaluate the bible on whether it establishes stakes that can survive escalation. Specifically:

**Is the engine capable of generating fresh stakes weekly** without repeating the same threat. A show whose only stake is one person's life runs out in six episodes.

**Do the stakes reach safety needs, or does the show run entirely on spiritual needs.** Comedies and relationship dramas legitimately live in the low-cost spiritual cell, and the great ones almost always sneak in a safety-need stake, freedom, livelihood, access to a child, which is exactly why they cut deeper than their peers. Check for it.

**Is there escalation room.** A bible that opens at maximum jeopardy has the same problem as a second act that opens with the boldest version of the plan. Ask what season three looks like.

**Do the ongoing characters have their own stakes**, or does everything hang on the lead. Long-form gives you room to build independent audience-stake bonds, which is the strongest version of the second modulator. Use it.

## Cost of participation across a season

The alignment rule holds and gets harder. Series regulars survive by contract, which flattens the perceived cost of physical danger over time. Compensate with costs the show can genuinely spend: livelihood, freedom, reputation, access to family, and the loss of relationships. These are non-fatal and permanent, which is what serialized storytelling is actually good at.

If the audience has learned the hero cannot be hurt, physical jeopardy has become decorative regardless of how it is staged. Say that plainly when you see it.

## Procedurals

A procedural is the sleuth story from `matrix.md`, repeated. It begins each episode in the low-cost spiritual cell, driven by justice, and it usually needs the same Act 1 supports every week: a connection between the investigator and the case, a bond with the victim, a deadline. The differentiator between a generic procedural and a good one is whether a serialized safety-need stake hovers underneath the weekly case, giving the low-cost work a background charge.

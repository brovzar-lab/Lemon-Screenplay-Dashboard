# Michael Arndt Endings Tool

A diagnostic, construction, and repair tool for the ENDING of a film or series, based on Michael Arndt's framework where a great ending is not felt, it is engineered: three sets of stakes converging in a two-minute climax where underdog values overturn dominant values.

Arndt's own disclaimer applies and should be honored: this is one specific model, not a law. He is describing how three films work, not prescribing how all stories must work. The "Insanely Great" model is a small subset of the great endings that exist. Use it as a precision instrument for a particular kind of ending, not as a verdict that every other ending is wrong.

---

## Core Principle

Most endings fail in the same place. The hero gets the girl and the money, and the audience thinks: so what? The plot resolved, but nothing meant anything. The reason is almost always the same: the writer handled the external stakes and the internal stakes, and neglected the third set -- the philosophical stakes. The philosophical stakes are where meaning lives, and **meaning is what supercharges emotion**. An ending without philosophical stakes can be competent and even surprising, but it cannot be transcendent.

The second thing most endings get wrong is geometry. Even when all three stakes exist, writers resolve them in three separate scenes -- the external climax on page 105, the emotional reconciliation on page 108, the thematic note on page 110 -- and the energy dissipates. The insanely great ending collapses all three resolutions into the same two minutes, the same physical space, the same single act. Each resolution amplifies the others. The audience does not experience three good endings. They experience one moment that means three things at once.

Plan the ending first. Pixar's saying, which Arndt carries: "the last thing we figure out is the First Act." The ending governs everything upstream. If you do not know your ending, the setup cannot plant the seeds the climax needs to harvest.

---

## The Quality Ladder

Every ending sits on one of three rungs. Name the rung before doing anything else.

- **BAD = positive + predictable.** The hero wins and you saw it coming. No surprise, no lasting impression.
- **GOOD = positive + surprising.** The hero wins in a way you did not predict, but it still obeys the story's logic. Satisfying. Forgettable a week later.
- **INSANELY GREAT = positive + surprising + meaningful.** The hero wins, you did not see it coming, and the victory inverts the moral order of the story's world. Emotion supercharged with meaning. This is the only rung where the audience carries something out of the theater.

"Positive" is Arndt's framing for the model, but insanely great does not mean happy. The same machine produces devastating endings (see 8 1/2 in the worked examples): the structure holds, but the underdog values lose, or win at unbearable cost, and the meaning lands just as hard. What separates the rungs is never the plot outcome. It is the presence or absence of a value contest resolving inside the climax.

## The Engine

This is the full Arndt mechanism. In every mode, you are working these components.

### Three Sets of Stakes

A story carries three independent stakes, and **each one has its own complete arc**: its own inciting incident, its own first-act break, its own deepening through Act 2, its own payoff at the climax. They are established in Act 1, raised in Act 2, and converge at the end.

- **External stakes.** What is concretely gained or lost in the outside world: survival, money, the contest, the bomb, the deadline, the achievable goal. The visible plot question -- will they or won't they.
- **Internal / emotional stakes.** What is gained or lost in the heart: romantic love, parent-child love, friendship, self-respect, a call to greatness answered or refused. The relationship and the self.
- **Philosophical stakes.** A contest between two competing value systems. The most neglected, the most important, the one that creates meaning. This is not a message. It is a structural force as powerful as the external plot.

Most writers handle external and internal on instinct. The diagnostic edge of this tool is almost always the third set.

### Dominant vs Underdog Values

"Theme" is a uselessly vague word. Replace it with the actual mechanism: a contest between two value systems embedded in the story from page one.

- **Dominant values** are the governing ethos of the story's world. How power actually works there, what gets rewarded, what the system enforces. Usually efficient, cynical, strong, "realistic."
- **Underdog values** are the protagonist's alternative. More human, more vulnerable, less efficient, easy to dismiss as naive.

The meaning of the story IS the outcome of this contest. When the underdog values overturn the dominant values at the climax, the audience feels the world of the story change. That felt change is the emotion.

Examples of the contest:
- Star Wars: violence and coercion (dominant) vs trust in the Force (underdog).
- The Graduate: bourgeois conformity (dominant) vs raw authenticity (underdog).
- Little Miss Sunshine: competitive "winners and losers" (dominant) vs being yourself, refusing the game (underdog).

If you cannot name both value systems in a story, its ending will resolve plot without producing meaning. That is the most common and most fixable defect.

### The Cast of the Contest

Each set of stakes has its own antagonist and its own mentor. They are the spokespeople for the two value systems.

- **The Antagonist** is the most virulent embodiment of the dominant values. Their job is the **Antagonist's Aria**: a scene where they stop being an obstacle and become a philosopher, stating directly and persuasively why the dominant values are correct and the hero's are foolish. Tarkin: fear will keep the local systems in line. Mr. Potter in It's a Wonderful Life on why money is the only thing that matters. The aria must be good enough that the audience momentarily thinks the villain has a point. If the audience is never tempted, defeating the villain settles no argument.
- **The Mentor** is the embodiment of the underdog values, and gets the **Mentor's Aria**: the scene that voices the underdog truth and plants the seed the hero will hear again at the climax. Obi-Wan: the Force will be with you, always. At the decisive moment the hero "hears" the mentor and acts on the underdog values against their own self-interest.
- **The Closest Ally** has a **Judas moment**: a point where they choose the dominant values and abandon the underdog values the hero needs, deepening the despair. Han Solo leaving with his reward. Then the ally returns, having rejected the dominant values, and the return is folded into the convergent climax. Han swinging back to save Luke is the external, internal, and philosophical resolution compressed into one action.

### The Death Star of Your Story

The climax must happen in the worst place in the world: the physical location that is the apotheosis of the dominant values. The hero has to walk into the dragon's cave, the seat of the ethos they oppose.

- The Death Star is the worst place in the Star Wars universe -- pure violence and coercion made into architecture.
- The church wedding is the Death Star of bourgeois conformity in The Graduate.
- The beauty pageant is the Death Star of mindless competition in Little Miss Sunshine.

If a climax is staged somewhere neutral or convenient, it is leaving power on the table. Find where the dominant values reign supreme and stage the ending there.

### The Climax Mechanics

Three beats, compressed.

- **The Moment of Despair.** Just before the turn, all three stakes appear irretrievably lost. No positive outcome is visible. This is mandatory: the despair is the dark background that makes the reversal blaze. If the win comes easy, there is no contrast to amplify it.
- **The Decisive Act.** From inside the despair, the protagonist makes a single, active, decisive act that embraces the underdog values against their own self-interest. Luke switches off the targeting computer. Ben pounds on the glass and screams Elaine's name. The Hoover family climbs onto the stage and dances. The hero is not rescued by circumstance. The hero chooses, and the choice costs.
- **Convergence.** All three climaxes resolve in roughly two minutes, in as close proximity as possible, in logical connection with each other. The decisive act triggers the external resolution, the internal resolution, and the philosophical resolution at once. Engineering this convergence is, in Arndt's words, the hardest and most important structural problem in screenwriting. It is also the single thing that separates good from insanely great.

### The Emotional Promise

The opening of the story writes a check: it promises the audience a specific kind of emotional payoff. The ending has to cash that exact check, not a different one. Diagnose the promise the beginning makes, then test whether the ending pays off that promise or some unrelated one. A technically perfect ending that answers the wrong emotional question still feels hollow.

## Three Modes

Identify the mode from context. If genuinely unclear, ask once, then proceed.

### MODE 1: DIAGNOSE

**When:** A finished or drafted story, treatment, outline, or script is brought to find out why the ending does or does not land, and what to do about it. This is the default mode and the most common request.

**Process:**

1. **Locate the intended climax.** Find the single moment the whole story is built toward. If you cannot find one clear convergent moment -- if the story trails off, or resolves in scattered beats -- that absence is already a large part of the diagnosis. Name it.

2. **Extract the three sets of stakes.** For each (external, internal, philosophical): name what is gained or lost, and trace its arc. Is it set up in Act 1? Deepened in Act 2? Does it pay off at the climax? Mark each Working, Thin, or Missing. The philosophical set is missing or thin far more often than the other two -- check it hardest.

3. **Name the value contest.** State the dominant values and the underdog values in plain language. If both cannot be named from the material, flag it: this is usually the root cause of a "so what?" ending.

4. **Cast the contest.** Is there an antagonist who is the spokesperson for the dominant values, and do they get an aria (a moment of being persuasively, temptingly right)? Is there a mentor for the underdog values with an aria that plants the climactic seed? Is there a closest ally with a Judas moment and a return? Mark each present or absent.

5. **Find the Death Star.** Is the climax staged in the location that is the apotheosis of the dominant values, or somewhere neutral and convenient? Name the location it should happen in if it currently does not.

6. **Test the climax mechanics.** Is there a genuine Moment of Despair where all three stakes seem lost? Is there a single Decisive Act by the protagonist embracing underdog values against self-interest -- or does the ending resolve because of what happens TO the hero (passive resolution)? Do the three stakes converge in one tight action, or scatter across separate scenes?

7. **Check the Emotional Promise.** What did the opening promise emotionally? Does the ending pay off that specific promise?

8. **Score and prescribe.** Place it on the ladder (Bad / Good / Insanely Great) with the mechanical reason, then state the single highest-leverage move to raise it a rung.

**Output the diagnostic in this format:**

```
# Ending Diagnostic: [Title]

## Verdict: [BAD / GOOD / INSANELY GREAT]
[One line: the mechanical reason it lands here, and the highest-leverage move to raise it a tier.]

## The Three Sets of Stakes
- External: [what's at stake] | Setup/Deepen/Payoff: [✓/✗ each] | [Working / Thin / Missing]
- Internal: [what's at stake] | [...] | [Working / Thin / Missing]
- Philosophical: [the value contest] | [...] | [Working / Thin / Missing]

## The Value Contest
- Dominant values: [the governing ethos of this world]
- Underdog values: [the protagonist's alternative]
- Does the audience feel the world change when underdog wins? [Yes / No / Not yet -- why]

## The Cast of the Contest
- Antagonist (dominant spokesperson): [who] -- Aria? [present / absent: do they ever get to be persuasively right?]
- Mentor (underdog spokesperson): [who] -- Aria? [present / absent: is the climactic seed planted?]
- Closest ally: [who] -- Judas moment? [Y/N] -- Return folded into climax? [Y/N]

## The Climax Mechanics
- The Death Star (worst place in the world): [is the climax staged in the apotheosis of dominant values? if not, where should it be?]
- Moment of Despair: [do all three stakes seem lost at once?]
- The Decisive Act: [is there one active choice by the hero, against self-interest, for underdog values? name it -- or flag passive resolution]
- Convergence: [do all three resolve in one tight moment, or scatter across scenes?]

## The Emotional Promise
- What the opening promises: [the emotional payoff the setup writes a check for]
- What the ending delivers: [does it cash that check, or a different one?]

## Diagnosis
[The real problem, named precisely. Not "the ending is weak" but the mechanical cause: missing philosophical stakes / unnamed value contest / scattered climax / passive resolution / wrong location / toothless antagonist / broken emotional promise / no despair.]

## The Fix (highest leverage first)
[Concrete moves in priority order. The first move alone should be enough to raise it a rung.]
```

### MODE 2: BUILD

**When:** A premise, idea, or partial story exists but has no ending yet, or the ending is a blank. Build the climax from the model, then work backward to the setup.

**Process:**

1. **Start at the end.** Do not build forward. Decide what the climax is before anything else.

2. **Find the value contest first.** Ask: what does the world of this story believe (dominant values)? What does the protagonist come to embody against it (underdog values)? Everything else hangs off this. If the contest is unclear, interrogate the premise until both systems are named.

3. **Define the three stakes around the contest:**
   - External: the concrete, achievable goal whose pursuit is the visible plot.
   - Internal: the relationship or self-respect line that runs underneath.
   - Philosophical: the value contest, now stated as what is gained or lost in the moral order if underdog values win or lose.
   - Confirm each one can carry its own arc across three acts.

4. **Cast the contest.** Assign the antagonist (dominant spokesperson) and write the shape of their aria -- the persuasive case for the dominant values. Assign the mentor (underdog spokesperson) and the seed their aria plants. If the structure allows, give the closest ally a Judas moment and a return.

5. **Choose the Death Star.** Name the location that is the apotheosis of the dominant values, and stage the climax there.

6. **Engineer the convergence.** Design the Moment of Despair (all three lost), the Decisive Act (one choice, against self-interest, for underdog values), and the two-minute window in which the act triggers external + internal + philosophical resolution together. This is the hard part. Spend the most effort here.

7. **Work backward to Act 1.** Now seed the setup: the emotional promise the opening must make, and the three inciting incidents (one per stake) that the climax will pay off. State what has to be planted for the harvest to work.

8. **Deliver the ending blueprint** using the diagnostic format above, written as prescriptions instead of findings.

### MODE 3: ELEVATE

**When:** An ending already works at the Bad or Good rung and needs to be engineered up toward Insanely Great. Usually run straight after a DIAGNOSE.

**Process:**

1. **Confirm the rung and the weakest link.** Restate where the ending sits and the single mechanical reason it is not a rung higher. Verify before building. Fixing the wrong link wastes pages.

2. **Work the root, not the symptom.** Ending problems cascade. A flat climax is usually a symptom of a missing value contest, which is usually a symptom of an antagonist who is just "evil" instead of persuasive. Trace upstream:
   - Climax means nothing emotionally → is the philosophical stake present, or only external + internal?
   - Philosophical stake feels absent → are the dominant and underdog values actually named and dramatized, or just implied?
   - Value contest feels weightless → does the antagonist get a real aria the audience is tempted by?
   - Victory feels unearned → is there a true Moment of Despair before it?
   - Win feels hollow → is the hero making a decisive act, or being saved by circumstance?
   - Energy dissipates → are the three resolutions converging, or scattered?
   - Right machine, wrong feeling → does the ending pay off the emotional promise the opening made?

3. **Generate concrete options.** For each broken component, give two or three specific moves, not vague notes. Not "raise the stakes" but the actual scene, location, line, or choice:

```
### [Component]: [what's broken]

The problem: [precise mechanical statement]

Option A: [a concrete move -- a scene, a reveal, a staging change, a decisive act. What happens, where, what it costs, what it resolves.]

Option B: [a different move serving the same structural function. Different mechanism, same job.]

Option C: [third option if useful.]

Convergence check: whichever option, does it land inside the same two-minute window as the other resolutions, or does it need to be pulled closer?
```

4. **Run the convergence pass last.** Once the components are fixed individually, the final move is almost always compression: pulling the external, internal, and philosophical resolutions into the same moment and the same act. State explicitly what has to move where.

## Applying the Value Contest to Mexican / LatAm Material

The model is universal, but the value systems are culture-specific, and naming them precisely for this market is where the meaning gets sharp. The dominant values in a Mexican or LatAm story are often: money and narco-power, machismo, family honor and obligation, class and who is owed deference, institutional corruption as the way things simply work, the patriarch's word. The underdog values the protagonist embodies against them are the meaning of the piece.

Worked angle on existing slate logic:
- An avocado-empire / cartel saga (Succession meets Narcos): dominant values are blood, money, and the family that eats its own; underdog values might be the one heir who refuses the logic of violence, or chooses an outsider over the dynasty. The climax belongs in the seat of the empire (the Death Star), and the meaning is whether that refusal survives contact with it.
- A horror premise where activists are hunted by a butcher in a supermarket: dominant values are consumption, the slaughterhouse logic that some bodies are meat; underdog values are the refusal to become a predator to survive. The Decisive Act is whether the final survivor kills on the butcher's terms or wins on their own.
- A period institutional drama (women in a corrupt male system): dominant values are the institution's authority and the disposability of the people it polices; underdog values are solidarity and truth. The aria belongs to the man who runs the institution and genuinely believes it must work this way.

Always name both value systems in the actual cultural register of the story, not in the abstract. "Corruption vs honesty" is thin. "The cartel pays for the school and the hospital, so who are you to call it evil" is an Antagonist's Aria.

## Common Ending Failures

Flag these on sight. They are the recurring causes.

- **The "so what?" ending.** External and internal resolved, no philosophical stakes. Hero gets the girl and the money and the audience shrugs. The most common defect; the fix is always to find and dramatize the latent value contest.
- **No value contest.** There is no dominant ethos for the underdog values to overturn, so the win settles nothing. Nothing in the world changes.
- **Toothless antagonist.** The villain is "evil," never persuasive, gets no aria. Beating them resolves a threat but not an argument, so the victory carries no meaning.
- **Passive climax.** The ending resolves because of what happens TO the hero, not a decisive act BY the hero against self-interest. The hero must choose, and it must cost.
- **Scattered climax.** Three stakes resolved in three separate scenes. No convergence. The energy leaks out between resolutions instead of compounding.
- **Wrong location.** Climax staged somewhere neutral or convenient instead of the Death Star, the apotheosis of the dominant values. Power left unused.
- **No despair.** The win comes too easy. With no Moment of Despair where all three seem lost, the reversal has no dark background to blaze against.
- **Broken promise.** The ending pays off a different emotion than the one the opening promised. The machine works; it answers the wrong question.
- **Theme as a message.** A moral stated in dialogue instead of dramatized as a value contest with a won-or-lost outcome. Speeches are not stakes.
- **Predictable victory.** Positive but unsurprising. Stuck on the Bad rung purely because the audience saw it coming; needs a genuine reversal, not just more feeling.

## Integration With Other Skills

This tool owns the ending and the climax specifically -- the last two minutes and the architecture that makes them pay. For adjacent work:

- **Full three-act structure** → use `save-the-cat` or `story-grid-expert`. Snyder's Five-Point Finale is the convergence zone; his All Is Lost / Dark Night of the Soul beat IS Arndt's Moment of Despair. Story Grid's Core Event and value charge map directly onto the dominant/underdog contest. Run those for the whole shape; run this for whether the ending lands.
- **Character arc** → use `km-weiland`. Weiland's Lie/Need is the internal stakes line; her climactic choice is Arndt's Decisive Act. The antagonist who embodies the Lie is the same figure as Arndt's dominant-values spokesperson. Diagnose the arc there, diagnose the ending here.
- **Moral argument and genre beats** → use `truby-expert`. Truby's "moral argument" is the same animal as Arndt's philosophical stakes; his "battle" and "self-revelation" are the convergence and the Decisive Act. Use Truby when the genre demands specific obligatory beats this model leaves open.
- **Causal logic of the climax** → use `therefore-but`. Convergence only works if the Decisive Act CAUSES the three resolutions rather than merely preceding them. Run a therefore/but pass on the climax sequence to confirm the act drives the payoff.
- **Series finales and pilot endings** → use `grisanti-series-development` or `grisanti-pilot-analysis`. Grisanti's dilemma and Triangle of the Wound operate at series level; this model applies to the pilot's ending and to the season or series climax. The dominant/underdog contest is the show's engine restated.
- **Writing the actual climax pages** → use `co-writer`, `story-ninja`, or `pluma-mexicana`. This tool sets the architecture. Once the convergence is designed, hand off to the writing tools for execution and the Mexican line-level finish.
- **Horror endings** → use `horror-screenwriter` alongside this. The genre's obligatory final confrontation is a natural Death Star; the survivor's last choice is a natural Decisive Act.
- **Building the cast of the contest** → use `enneagram-architect` (from scratch) or `enneagram-analyst` (existing). An antagonist built as the apotheosis of dominant values often comes straight from a clear Enneagram type at a low level of health.
- **The value contest as the sell** → use `charlie-designer`. The dominant-vs-underdog contest is usually the marketing hook in one line. If the ending has real philosophical stakes, the campaign has a spine.

## Appendix: Worked Examples

Arndt's canonical endings in compact form. Use them as pattern templates: find the one closest in shape to the material at hand, map the components across, and wherever a slot is full in the template but empty in the material, that gap is the diagnosis. The cast structure flexes -- not every film has a clean mentor, and in one case the Antagonist's Aria comes from the hero's own ally.

**STAR WARS: A NEW HOPE (1977).** The cleanest three-way convergence.
- Stakes: External = destroy the Death Star, save the Rebellion. Internal = Luke the orphan finds a family and himself, plus Han's friendship. Philosophical = coercion and technology vs faith in the Force.
- Values: Dominant = rule by fear and force (Tarkin's aria: "Fear will keep the local systems in line"). Underdog = trust the Force (Obi-Wan's aria: trust your feelings, "let go").
- Death Star: the Death Star itself, violence as architecture.
- Despair: trench run, Vader locked on, the computer the only apparent tool, seconds from annihilation.
- Decisive Act: Luke switches off the targeting computer and trusts the Force, against self-interest.
- Ally Judas + return: Han takes his reward and leaves (money over cause), then swings back to clear Vader.
- Convergence (~2 min): hears Obi-Wan (philosophical seed) → computer off (decisive act) → Han returns and clears Vader (ally + internal) → Luke fires on instinct, port hit (external) → station explodes. Medal ceremony pays off the internal.

**THE GRADUATE (1967).** Bends the cast: no clean mentor; Ben becomes the underdog value through raw action.
- Stakes: External = stop Elaine's wedding, win her, the race to the church. Internal = Ben's love for Elaine and his search for an authentic self. Philosophical = bourgeois conformity vs authentic feeling.
- Values: Dominant = the affluent establishment, the correct marriage, the script written for a young man's life. Underdog = following your own desire against the script, even when mortifying.
- Antagonist aria: Mrs. Robinson and the parents' certainty about who belongs with whom.
- Death Star: the church wedding, the sacrament of the correct union.
- Despair: Ben arrives too late, vows said, locked behind glass on the balcony.
- Decisive Act: he pounds the glass and screams "ELAINE!" in the temple of decorum; she chooses him; he bars the door with a cross.
- Convergence: the scream (authenticity vs conformity) → Elaine chooses him (external + internal) → escape onto the bus. The final shot, elation curdling to uncertainty, is the philosophical stake refusing a cheap resolution.

**LITTLE MISS SUNSHINE (2006).** Arndt's own. The external LOSS is the philosophical WIN.
- Stakes: External = get Olive to the pageant and compete. Internal = a fractured family healing, each carrying a private failure. Philosophical = "winners and losers" success ideology vs being yourself and refusing the game.
- Values: Dominant = competitive success, ranking, the sexualized pageant culture. Underdog = love your family, refuse to be ashamed of losing, the people on the bus are what matter.
- Antagonist aria: Richard's winner/loser doctrine, plus the pageant culture made flesh.
- Mentor aria: Grandpa -- a real loser is someone too afraid to try; he choreographs her dance, plants the seed, then dies on the trip.
- Death Star: the pageant stage.
- Despair: Olive's burlesque routine horrifies the crowd; an official demands they stop her; public humiliation in real time.
- Decisive Act: Richard the zealot climbs onstage and dances with her; the whole family joins, embracing the loss.
- Convergence: family joins the dance (internal + philosophical: they reject the winner/loser religion) → officials erupt, family hauled off and banned (external loss). The loss IS the win.

**TOY STORY 3 (2010).** Arndt's own. Holds one of cinema's great despair beats.
- Stakes: External = escape the incinerator, survive Andy leaving. Internal = Woody's loyalty, fear of obsolescence, the toys' love. Philosophical = love means belonging and letting go vs the nihilism that you are trash to be discarded.
- Values: Dominant = Lotso's worldview (aria at the dump: "We're all just trash, waiting to be thrown away"). Underdog = loyalty, togetherness, love that survives being outgrown.
- Death Star: the incinerator at the landfill, "you are garbage" made literal.
- Despair: the toys slide toward the fire, fail to climb out, then hold hands and accept the end together.
- Decisive Act: togetherness in the face of death (the held hands), then Woody's note giving the toys to Bonnie, Andy letting go.
- Convergence: the donation and last play session -- external (the toys find a future), internal (Andy and Woody's goodbye), philosophical (you are not trash, love means letting go). "So long, partner."

**IT'S A WONDERFUL LIFE (1946).** Included for the definitive Antagonist's Aria.
- Mr. Potter argues with total conviction that money and property are all that matter, that George is a failure who threw his life away on others, and -- the knife -- that George is "worth more dead than alive." The dominant value at its most ruthless and most tempting. Because the audience half-believes him, George's rediscovery of his life's worth carries real weight rather than mere sentiment.

**8 1/2 (1963).** Proof the model produces tragic, inward endings too, and the supreme case of the aria coming from the hero's ally.
- Guido cannot make his film, is alienated from his wife, cannot assemble a coherent story of his own life. The Antagonist's Aria is spoken not by a villain but by his own screenwriter and closest critical ally: better not to make the movie at all, that if an artist cannot make something perfect it is nobler to stay silent. The machine is intact -- value contest, aria, despair -- but the resolution is ambiguous and inward. Insanely great is not a synonym for a happy ending. It is a synonym for an ending where a contest of values resolves and the resolution means something.

**Matching shapes:** External plot dominant with clean three-way convergence → Star Wars. The external loss that is the philosophical win → Little Miss Sunshine. Authenticity vs conformity with an ambiguous final image → The Graduate. Letting go and mortality, acceptance earning the turn → Toy Story 3. Tragic or inward resolution that still means something → 8 1/2.

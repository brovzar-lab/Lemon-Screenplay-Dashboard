# Grisanti Assessment Framework

Detailed questions and diagnostics for developing TV series.

## The Core Question

Before any other development: **What does your central character want?**

If you cannot answer this clearly, the series cannot work.

---

## Log Line Diagnosis

### Formula
WHO (empathy) + DILEMMA (impossible choice) + ACTION (what they do) + GOAL (with irony)

### Examples

**Breaking Bad**: "When Walt, a high school chemistry teacher with a wife and a handicapped son, is given a diagnosis of terminal cancer, he turns to what he knows best, chemistry, and decides to make and distribute meth in order to have something to leave his family."

- WHO: High school chemistry teacher with wife and handicapped son
- DILEMMA: Terminal cancer diagnosis
- ACTION: Decides to make and distribute meth
- GOAL: Have something to leave his family

**Mad Men**: "Donald Draper, a conflicted ad man in the 1960's, must figure out how to keep the secret that the picture he paints and the world he creates is not from an identity that is his own in order to remain at the top of his game."

**The Good Wife**: "A betrayed housewife must pick up the pieces after her husband's very public and political scandal lands him in jail and she returns to the workforce to secure her family's future."

### Three Logline Levels

**Series Logline**: The big-picture logline for the entire series.
**Pilot Logline**: A summary of the A story in the pilot episode.
**Internal Logline**: The protagonist's emotional journey — internal conflict, how it drives decisions, how it connects to the external goal.

Plus B and C story loglines.

### If the Log Line Fails

Ask:
- Is the WHO creating empathy?
- Is the DILEMMA actually a dilemma (neither option good)?
- Is the ACTION clear?
- Is there irony in the GOAL?

A broken log line = a broken story. Fix this first.

---

## Dilemma Assessment

### What Makes a Powerful Dilemma

A dilemma offers at least two solutions, none practically acceptable. "Being on the horns of a dilemma" — neither horn comfortable.

**Breaking Bad example**:
- If Walt deals meth: risks jail, but has money for family
- If Walt doesn't deal: dies feeling he didn't provide

BOTH sides must be established. If only one side is clear, it's not a dilemma.

### Diagnostic Questions

1. What are the two (or more) options?
2. Why is NEITHER option good?
3. Do we understand why the character is torn?
4. What's the worst that happens with each choice?
5. Which does the character choose, and why?

---

## Goal Assessment

### External vs Internal

**External Goal**: What they want to achieve (visible, plot-driven)
**Internal Goal**: Why it matters to them emotionally (invisible, character-driven)

Both must be clear.

### Diagnostic Questions

1. What is the external goal?
2. What is the internal goal (why do they want it)?
3. Is the goal clear by end of Act I?
4. Does the goal stem from the dilemma?
5. What is the WORST that happens if they don't achieve it?
6. Is the character ACTIVE (not reactive) in pursuing it?

### Goal-Resonance Test

For every scene, ask: "How does this connect to the goal?"

If you can't answer, the scene may not belong.

---

## Stakes Assessment

### The 20-Minute Rule

Within 20 minutes, the audience must know: **Why do I care?**

If they don't, they have 200+ other channels.

### Questions to Ask

1. What is at stake externally? (plot)
2. What is at stake internally? (emotion)
3. Have both been clearly established?
4. Do the stakes escalate throughout?
5. What's the WORST possible outcome?
6. Do we understand why THIS matters to THIS character?

### Stakes Arc Mapping

Map how stakes escalate through the pilot:
- **Teaser/Act 1 Stakes**: Initial establishment
- **Act 2 Stakes**: Escalation
- **Act 3 Stakes**: Highest point (All Is Lost)
- **Act 4 Stakes**: Resolution plus new stakes for the series

### If Stakes Feel Low

- Go deeper into the internal goal
- Make the worst outcome worse
- Connect the goal to something universal
- Show what the character has to lose

## Act Out Assessment

For each act break, answer:

1. What is the story beat?
2. Is this an: obstacle / escalating obstacle / turning point / "all is lost" moment?
3. Does it reflect back to the initial goal?
4. Does it create anticipation for what's next?
5. Does it end on the A story (most act outs should)?

### Common Act Out Problems

- **No connection to goal**: Act out feels random or disconnected
- **No escalation**: Obstacles stay at same intensity
- **Reactive character**: Things happen TO the character, not because of their action
- **Wrong story**: Ending on B or C story when A story has the momentum

---

## Active vs Reactive Character

The central character must be ACTIVE in achieving/solving the goal.

### Signs of Reactive Character

- Things happen TO them
- Other characters drive the plot
- They make discoveries rather than choices
- They respond rather than initiate
- The resolution comes from outside (deus ex machina)

### Fix

- Give them agency in every major decision
- Make them cause obstacles through their actions
- Let THEIR choice create the "all is lost" moment
- Ensure THEY actively solve the final goal

## Character Arc: Five Questions

These form the emotional spine of the series. If you can't answer them, the character isn't ready.

1. **What is the childhood wound of your central character?** — A past trauma or formative event that happened BEFORE we enter the story, shaping their internal struggles, beliefs, and behavior patterns.

2. **How does the Series Trigger and Dilemma split open this wound?** — The series conflict should reactivate the character's deepest emotional pain. This is what makes the story feel urgent and personal — it's not just a plot problem, it's an emotional reckoning.

3. **What is the negative narrative that comes from these wounds?** — The self-defeating beliefs or assumptions the character holds because of their past. This is the lie they tell themselves. Examples: "I'm not worthy of love," "If I show weakness, I'll be destroyed," "Everyone I trust will betray me."

4. **What is the flaw that comes from the negative narrative?** — How the internal lie manifests in actions and decisions. The flaw is VISIBLE behavior the audience can see — the thing that makes the character their own worst enemy.

5. **How does the flaw/negative narrative get in the way of achieving the goal?** — The engine of internal conflict. The character's own psychology creates obstacles in their pursuit of the goal. External plot and internal struggle should be in tension throughout.

## Voice Assessment

Voice = the writer's gold. It comes from fictionalizing truth.

### Where to Find Voice

Best places in a pilot:
1. The "all is lost" moment — what is the character made of?
2. Moments of extreme emotion
3. Dialogue that says what we'd never say out loud
4. Universal themes the writer has lived

The strongest places to reveal voice are Acts 3 and 4 — the escalating obstacle and the "all is lost" moment. When the character is at their lowest, that's when we learn what they're truly made of.

### Diagnostic Questions

1. Where does the script feel most authentic?
2. Where does it feel generic or "TV-like"?
3. What life experience is the writer drawing from?
4. Where could the character go deeper emotionally?
5. What would the character NEVER say — but should?

### If Voice Is Missing

- Look for the "all is lost" moment — this is where voice should emerge
- Ask what the writer personally connects to in this story
- Encourage going further than comfortable
- Find the universal theme from real experience

## Series Potential Assessment

### The Season 4 Test

Can you imagine what this show looks like in Season 4?

### Diagnostic Questions

1. Is there a strong engine for endless story?
2. Is the world/franchise clear and sustainable?
3. What does episode #100 look like?
4. Does the pilot show what the series will be (not just backstory)?
5. When does the series actually START in the pilot?

### If Series Potential Is Weak

- Strengthen the world/franchise
- Create more avenues for story
- Move backstory later, series earlier ("Move Act IV to Act I")
- Ensure the engine generates conflict naturally

## Common Development Problems

### Problem: Too Much Story, Not Enough Character
**Fix**: Slow down, let us understand who these people are

### Problem: Too Much Character, Not Enough Story
**Fix**: Characters reveal through action — put them in conflict

### Problem: Confusing Storylines
**Fix**: Simplify — make sure each story has clear dilemma → goal → resolution

### Problem: Backstory Dominates
**Fix**: "Move Act IV to Act I" — start the series earlier, reveal backstory throughout

### Problem: Series Starts in Last Act
**Fix**: The pilot should demonstrate the series, not just set it up

### Problem: "Why Do I Care?" Unanswered
**Fix**: Establish stakes earlier, make the worst outcome clearer

## Note-Giving Framework

### Structure Each Note

1. **What's working** (and why — give the principle)
2. **What's not working** (and why — give the principle)
3. **Specific fix** (actionable, concrete)
4. **Question to consider** (helps them think deeper)

### Executive vs Instructional

**Executive note**: "The act out on page 32 doesn't land."
**Instructional note**: "The act out needs to reflect back to the initial goal to maintain momentum."
**Combined**: "The act out on page 32 doesn't land because it doesn't reflect back to the initial goal. Consider: what obstacle to the goal would create the most anticipation for what's next?"

### Never

- Give notes that contradict the goal/dilemma
- Suggest fixes that change the fundamental story
- Focus on dialogue before structure is solid
- Give notes without explaining why

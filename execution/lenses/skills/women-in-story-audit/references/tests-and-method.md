# Tests and Evidence Method

Research checked: 2026-08-24. Use original or institutional sources where available. These tests are lenses with different scopes, not interchangeable certifications.

## Core Tests

### Bechdel-Wallace Test

Original rule: the work has at least two women who talk to each other about something other than a man.

- The rule appeared in Alison Bechdel's 1985 *Dykes to Watch Out For* strip, "The Rule," and Bechdel credits Liz Wallace with the idea.
- Requiring both women to be named is a common later convention, not part of the original strip. Report whether the result uses the original or named-character convention.
- It measures a minimal pattern of presence and interaction. It does not measure agency, centrality, interiority, duration, intersectionality, quality, or feminism.
- Source: [Alison Bechdel's official archive](https://dykestowatchoutfor.com/the-rule/)

### Mako Mori Test

A work has at least one female character who receives her own narrative arc that is not about supporting a man's story.

- Tumblr user Chaila proposed it in 2013 during discussion of *Pacific Rim* and the limits of Bechdel-Wallace for a significant East Asian woman character.
- It complements Bechdel-Wallace; it does not replace women-to-women relationships.
- "Own arc" requires a meaningful change or progression driven by the character's problem, desire, decisions, and consequences. Competence or plot participation alone is not an arc.
- Source: [documented original wording and provenance](https://geekfeminism.fandom.com/wiki/Mako_Mori_test)

### Ko Test

A film has a non-white, female-identifying person who speaks in five or more scenes and speaks English.

- Writer and actor Naomi Ko contributed the test to FiveThirtyEight's 2017 *Next Bechdel Test* project.
- It measures sustained spoken presence for a woman of color, not agency, depth, authenticity, or narrative importance.
- The English criterion addresses a particular U.S. media pattern. Do not use it as a universal quality standard or treat accented English as a defect. For non-English-language projects, report the historical result if requested, then explain why it may not fit the work.
- Sources: [The Ko Test](https://thekotest.com/), [FiveThirtyEight project data](https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/data/tree/master/next-bechdel)

### Villarreal Test

Lindsey Villarreal's test initially fails a movie if its lead woman is introduced as sexualized; hardened, expressionless, or soulless; or a tired, older, or overworked matriarch.

The film may "redeem" that introduction when the lead later has at least three of four characteristics: a career with authority or power; motherhood; recklessness or bad decisions; and sexuality or a sexual identity she owns.

- This is not a general test of agency or distinct personality.
- The categories are subjective and culturally contingent. The redemption traits are neither necessary nor sufficient for dimensional characterization, so report the formal result and then perform the broader craft analysis.
- Do not confuse it with Ligiah Villalobos's separate Latina-representation test.
- Sources: [FiveThirtyEight project data](https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/data/tree/master/next-bechdel), [preserved project definitions](https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/cs/cs106a/cs106a.1222/section-w2021/section8/section8.html)

## Optional Lenses

Apply only when they answer the user's question or clarify a core-test blind spot.

### Peirce Test

Ask whether a female protagonist or antagonist has her own story; authentic needs and desires pursued through dramatic action; and actions the audience can understand or empathize with. Use it to examine agency and motive, not to require likability.

### Sexy Lamp Test

Kelly Sue DeConnick's thought experiment asks whether a woman could be replaced by an attractive object without materially changing the plot. Use it to detect a character functioning only as an object, reward, clue, or motivation. Plot indispensability still does not prove interiority.

### Sphinx Test

Use the Sphinx Theatre questions as a development lens: Is a woman central, active rather than merely reactive, consequential to the action, connected to other women, non-stereotypical, complex, and compelling? This is particularly useful during creation and rewrite, not as a certification stamp.

### Villalobos Test

Use only for a requested Latina-representation lens. Ligiah Villalobos's historical criteria concern a Latina lead plus professional or educated Latina representation, English without an accent, and lack of sexualization. Treat the accent criterion as historical context, not a modern quality rule. Analyze whether language or accent is used to signal inferiority, foreignness, or comic ridicule.

### Vito Russo Test

For queer women and other LGBTQ characters, GLAAD asks whether a character is identifiably LGBTQ, is not defined solely by that identity, and matters to the plot. GLAAD explicitly notes that passing does not guarantee a non-problematic portrayal.

- Source: [GLAAD Vito Russo Test](https://glaad.org/sri/2020/vito-russo-test/)

## Perspective and Craft Evidence

Named tests mostly measure presence or structure. A useful screenplay audit also asks:

- **Scene ownership:** whose immediate objective, uncertainty, discovery, or emotional turn organizes the scene?
- **Causal power:** which decisions create later events rather than merely react to them?
- **Subjective access:** does the audience receive her private knowledge, sensory attention, interpretation, memory, fantasy, contradiction, or aftermath?
- **Relational range:** does she have different dynamics with women, men, family, rivals, colleagues, and community, or only one functional relationship?
- **Voice distribution:** how many scenes contain her speech, who she speaks with, which subjects she can introduce, and whether she carries important information or decisions?
- **Portrayal:** is description disproportionately focused on attractiveness, age, body, likability, caregiving, or emotional labor compared with comparable characters?
- **Cost and consequence:** can she make mistakes, cause damage, change strategy, refuse expectations, and live with outcomes?
- **Narrative sympathy:** does the story invite understanding of her experience even when she is wrong, difficult, comic, frightening, or morally compromised?

Use page and scene evidence. Counts can reveal patterns but cannot explain meaning by themselves.

## Intersectionality

Kimberle Crenshaw's framework explains that gender does not operate separately from race and other structures. Do not treat women as a single interchangeable category. Examine overlapping identities only when the material establishes them, and do not assume one character can represent an entire group.

- Source: [Crenshaw, *Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex*](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/uclf/vol1989/iss1/8/)

## Institutional Practice

The Geena Davis Institute's script work combines prominence and dialogue analysis with expert review of stereotypes, harmful language, cultural accuracy, and multiple identities while stating that artistic integrity matters. This supports a mixed method: quantitative observations plus contextual human craft analysis.

- Source: [Geena Davis Institute, Spellcheck for Bias](https://geenadavisinstitute.org/services/)

Keep authorship and hiring separate. Women writers, directors, producers, and crew can be evaluated from verified credits or plans, but those facts do not replace analysis of the story on the page.

## Evidence Status

Use:

- `Pass`: all formal criteria are supported.
- `Fail`: at least one formal criterion is clearly not met.
- `Indeterminate`: the material or identity evidence is ambiguous or incomplete.
- `Not assessable`: the format does not contain the evidence the test requires.

Never turn these statuses into a combined score. End with strengths, concerns, unknowns, and revision opportunities.

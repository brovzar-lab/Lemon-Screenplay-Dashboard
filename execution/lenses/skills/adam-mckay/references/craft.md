# Craft: The Improv Engine

This is how I actually build comedy at the scene level. It all comes from Del Close and the years in Chicago and at the UCB. When Billy asks for the comedy mechanics, this is the source.

## The game of the scene

This is the single most important concept I have. Everything else is downstream of it.

A scene has a game when there is one unusual thing at its center that the scene keeps returning to and escalating. Not a string of jokes. One engine. The discovery of the game is the first half of the scene. The heightening of it is the second half.

How to find the game:
1. Play the scene straight until something unusual, off, or unexpectedly true appears. That's the "first unusual thing."
2. Name it. What is the pattern? "This man will read literally anything off a teleprompter." "These two grown men have the emotional lives of nine-year-olds." "This anchor cannot process a woman being his equal."
3. Now you have the game. Every subsequent beat should be a heightening of that one thing, explored from a new angle. You are not adding new jokes. You are turning the same screw, harder.

A scene without a game is just events. It might have jokes in it, but it won't build, and it won't be memorable. When a comedy scene is dying, ninety percent of the time it's because nobody found the game and the scene is just plot with quips on top.

## Heightening

Once you have the game, you heighten. Two ways, and you usually braid them:

**Heighten by escalation.** Same pattern, bigger stakes each time. The teleprompter gag isn't funny once. It's funny when it goes from a harmless sign-off to "Go f*** yourself, San Diego" broadcast to the entire city. Each instance raises the cost.

**Heighten by exploration.** "Tell me the story of this unusual thing." Don't just make it bigger, make it deeper. If grown men act like children, explore every corner of that: the bunk beds, the job interview, the drum kit, the fight, the sleepwalking. You're mapping the full world the game implies.

The walk-on principle from improv: once the unusual thing is established, everyone who enters the scene should reinforce it, not question it. The world bends toward the game. In film terms, the supporting cast and the universe itself should treat the absurd premise as normal. That's "joining Team Fool." The moment a character stands outside the game and points at how weird it is, you've broken it.

## Total commitment

Absurdity only works at one hundred percent sincerity. This is non-negotiable.

The actor cannot signal that they know it's funny. Brick Tamland says "I love lamp" as a genuine confession of love. Ricky Bobby does not know he is ridiculous. The more outrageous the idea, the more deadly the commitment has to be. The instant a performance winks at the audience, the spell breaks and it stops being funny.

This is why I cast the way I cast and shoot the way I shoot. I need people who can be completely, soulfully serious inside an insane situation. When I'm giving notes on a comedy that isn't landing, "the actor is commenting instead of committing" is one of the first things I check.

## The absurdist toolbox

When the project is pure comedy, these are the specific moves I reach for. They all sit on top of the game.

**Deadpan delivery of the insane.** The funnier and weirder the content, the flatter and more sincere the delivery. The character treats the absurd as completely ordinary. The audience does the work of noticing how unhinged it is, and that gap is the laugh.

**Escalation into chaos.** Start a scene grounded and recognizable, find the game, then push it past realism into full anarchy, and crucially, never have a character step outside to comment on how crazy it got. The news team rumble in Anchorman is a petty rivalry that becomes a weapons-grade street battle, and everyone treats it as a normal Tuesday. The non-acknowledgment is what sells it.

**The runner.** Plant something stupid and small early. Bring it back later, bigger. Bring it back again, bigger and dumber. The audience feeling smart for catching the callback is half the pleasure.

**The non-sequitur as character.** A line that appears to come from nowhere but is actually a perfect X-ray of the person's psychology. These feel improvised even when written. They're the opposite of a joke that serves the plot, they serve the soul of the idiot.

**The confident-wrong engine.** Most of my leads are men of enormous certainty and no competence. The comedy is the distance between how they see themselves and what's actually true, and that distance is also, quietly, the satire, because these are the men who tend to end up in charge of things.

**Swing for the fences, then trim.** The note I give myself and everyone else: pitch the biggest, dumbest, most extreme version first. It's easy to dial an idea back. It's almost impossible to dial a timid idea up. Go too far on purpose and find the ceiling, then come down to the funniest sustainable altitude.

## Be funny in the room

This is a working instruction, not a theory. You cannot find comedy by describing it. When Billy is working on a comedy, do the bits. Throw actual alt lines. Pitch three versions of the dumb thing. Write the insane line and let it stand there. If a scene needs a runner, invent the runner on the spot. Improv is a doing art, and the only way to know if something's funny is to say it out loud and see if it stands up. Generous first, yes-and the premise, build the big stupid version, and only then start cutting to the funniest real version.

Register check: this whole toolbox is for comedy. If the material is a drama or a dramedy, pull back hard. Look for the absurd human truth, yes, but stop clowning, never undercut a real moment for a joke, and let serious scenes be serious. Match the gear the material is in.



My process, concretely:

1. Write a real, full, structured script. The script is the floor. It carries the story, the emotional architecture, the game of each scene. You need it. People who improvise without a script make a mess.
2. On set, shoot the scripted version once or twice so it's in the can.
3. Then blow it up. Let the actors improvise the scene for a long time, sometimes an hour, generating alternate lines, alternate escalations, alternate everything. Build a deep catalog of "alts."
4. The edit is where the comedy actually gets found. My editor and I go through the catalog and assemble the funniest, truest version, which is frequently nothing like the page. On Anchorman 2 we cut a whole version of the film where every laugh was a different alt take. That's how much raw material there is.

The lesson for a writer: don't be precious about the page. The page is essential and also disposable. Write it like it's final, then treat it like a launchpad.

## Casting is most of the job

If I cast the right person, I can hand them almost nothing and they'll find something better than I'd write. Ferrell, Carell, Rudd, Reilly. The reason I work with the same people over and over is that comedy is built in the trust between people who've riffed together for years. That shorthand is worth more than a perfect script. Protect the ensemble. The friends, the foils, the one-step-dumber and one-step-smarter people around the lead are not props. Each one needs their own little engine.

## The lovable fool

I keep writing confident idiots and I'll keep doing it because they're inexhaustible and because the confidence is the comedy. But the rule is the camera loves them. We are never above them. Ron Burgundy is a vain, sexist disaster and the movie adores him while it skewers the culture that made him. The skewer is aimed at the system, the world, the type. The individual fool gets our affection. If you find yourself sneering at your protagonist, stop. Condescension kills comedy.

## Tone as a dial

I don't pick a genre and stay in it. The Big Short is comedy, then heist, then tragedy, and it slides between them without announcing it. Step Brothers is broad and then suddenly aching. The craft is knowing what temperature a given moment needs and being willing to change it fast. Refusing to be pinned to one genre is creative freedom, and it's also a smuggling technique: an audience that's laughing has its defenses down, which is exactly when you can land something true and painful.

## Diagnostic: why a comedy scene isn't working

Run these in order:
1. Is there a game? Can you name the one unusual thing? If not, that's the problem, full stop.
2. Are we heightening it, or repeating it? Repetition is not escalation.
3. Is the character committing, or commenting?
4. Does the world support the game, or is someone standing outside pointing at it?
5. Is the supporting cast generating their own comedy, or just feeding the lead?
6. Is there a real emotion underneath, or are we playing surface?
7. Is the scene afraid of going far enough? Usually the answer is to push it further than feels safe.

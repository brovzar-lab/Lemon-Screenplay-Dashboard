# Story Grid Expert for Film & Television

Shawn Coyne's Story Grid methodology — an editorial diagnostic tool that reveals whether a story "works" by examining its structural components, genre requirements, and value progressions. Adapted here for screenwriting.

## Core Philosophy

**Genre is a promise to the audience.** Every story makes an implicit contract about the experience it will deliver. Story Grid provides the tools to identify that contract, understand its requirements, and fulfill it in surprising and resonant ways.

A story works when it hooks the audience with a compelling Inciting Incident, builds progressive complications that escalate to the limits of human experience, pays off its genre's conventions and obligatory scenes, delivers climaxes that are surprising yet inevitable, and transforms characters irrevocably.

## The Editor's Six Core Questions

Before analyzing or developing any screenplay, answer these:

1. **What's the Genre?** — External and Internal Content Genres (use the Five-Leaf Clover)
2. **What are the conventions and obligatory scenes?** — Genre-specific must-haves
3. **What's the Point of View?** — Person, Tense, and Mode (Narrative Path)
4. **What are the objects of desire?** — Conscious/External want AND Subconscious/Internal need
5. **What's the Controlling Idea/Theme?** — `[VALUE] prevails/fails when [ACTION]`
6. **What is the Beginning Hook, Middle Build, and Ending Payoff?**

## The Five-Leaf Genre Clover

Every story makes choices from five genre categories simultaneously:

**Leaf 1 — TIME**: Short Form (<40 min) | Medium Form (40-60 min) | Long Form (90-180 min)
**Leaf 2 — STRUCTURE**: Arch-Plot (single active protagonist, external conflict, closed ending, linear time) | Mini-Plot (multiple/passive protagonists, internal conflict, open ending) | Anti-Plot (breaks rules, no causality required)
**Leaf 3 — STYLE**: Drama, Comedy, Horror, Thriller, Documentary, Musical, Literary, Cinematic, etc.
**Leaf 4 — REALITY**: Factualism | Realism | Fantasy (Human, Magical, Science Fiction) | Absurdism
**Leaf 5 — CONTENT** (most important): Determines what the story is fundamentally about

## Content Genres — The Four Core Framework

Every content genre is built on four interlocking elements. Identify all four before writing a word:

1. **Core Need** — The primal human need the protagonist must fulfill
2. **Core Value** — The value spectrum the story turns on (positive through negation of the negation)
3. **Core Emotion** — What the audience should feel throughout the journey
4. **Core Event** — The must-have climactic scene that brings Need, Value, and Emotion together

### External Content Genres (Conscious Object of Desire)

| Genre | Core Need | Core Value | Core Emotion | Core Event |
|-------|-----------|------------|--------------|------------|
| Action | Survival | Death → Life | Excitement | Hero at Mercy of Villain |
| War | Safety | Dishonor → Honor | Intrigue | The Big Battle |
| Horror | Safety | Damnation → Life | Fear | Victim at Mercy of Monster |
| Crime | Safety | Injustice → Justice | Intrigue | Exposure of the Criminal |
| Thriller | Safety | Damnation → Life | Excitement | Hero at Mercy of Villain |
| Western/Eastern | Individual Sovereignty | Subjugation → Freedom | Intrigue | The Big Showdown |
| Love | Connection | Hate → Love | Romance | Proof of Love |
| Performance | Esteem | Shame → Respect | Triumph | The Big Performance Scene |
| Society | Recognition | Impotence → Power | Intrigue | The Revolution |

### Internal Content Genres (Subconscious Object of Desire)

| Genre | Core Need | Core Value | Core Emotion | Core Event |
|-------|-----------|------------|--------------|------------|
| Status | Respect | Failure → Success | Admiration/Pity | The Big Choice |
| Morality | Self-transcendence | Selfishness → Altruism | Satisfaction/Contempt | The Big Choice Scene |
| Worldview | Self-actualization | Ignorance → Wisdom | Satisfaction/Pity | Climax of Worldview Shift |

## The Five Commandments of Storytelling

Every unit of story — from individual beats to the global story — MUST contain all five:

### 1. Inciting Incident
The event that upsets the life balance of the protagonist. Two types:
- **Causal** — Directly caused by a character's action
- **Coincidental** — Chance occurrence (more common at global level)

Four rules for the Global Inciting Incident: (a) genre-specific type required, (b) must create imbalance so severe the protagonist must act, (c) must contain something the protagonist doesn't fully understand, (d) must point toward the ending.

### 2. Progressive Complications
Escalating difficulties that increase conflict and raise stakes. The key test: **how difficult would it be for the character to reverse their decision?** Complications must build from reversible to irreversible, approaching the limits of human experience. Each complication is delivered through a **Turning Point** — either:
- **Active** — A character does something that changes the situation
- **Revelatory** — New information is revealed that changes everything

### 3. Crisis
The dilemma that requires a choice between two options. Only two kinds:
- **Best Bad Choice** — Both options lead to negative outcomes (choose the least bad)
- **Irreconcilable Goods** — Both options are positive, but choosing one precludes the other

If there's an easy or obvious path, the crisis is broken. Crisis questions and how characters answer them DEFINE character.

### 4. Climax
The precise moment the character ACTS on their crisis choice. The truth of character. Must happen ON STAGE for the protagonist — never reported offstage. Climaxes progress from minor to medium to large to life-changing across the story.

### 5. Resolution
The new equilibrium after the climax. Often underestimated. Don't just sum up — create a fable or metaphor that reinforces how the climax changed the character. The resolution is where a good story becomes memorable.

## Value Progressions

Every scene must TURN — shift from one value state to another on the story's core spectrum. The full value spectrum for any genre has four levels:

```
POSITIVE (+) → CONTRARY (+/-) → NEGATIVE (-) → NEGATION OF NEGATION (--)
```

| Genre | + | +/- | - | -- |
|-------|---|-----|---|----|
| Thriller | Life | Unconsciousness | Death | Damnation (fate worse than death) |
| Love | Love | Desire/Attraction | Indifference/Hate | Self-hatred masquerading as love |
| Crime | Justice | Unfairness | Injustice | Tyranny |
| Status | Success | Compromise | Failure | Selling Out |
| Worldview | Wisdom | Knowledge | Ignorance | Blind certainty |
| Morality | Altruism | Self-interest | Selfishness | Self-destruction disguised as virtue |

The global story must progress through these levels. BH moves value one level; MB moves it further; EP takes it to the end of the line.

## Units of Story (Nested Structure)

Stories are Russian dolls — each unit contains the Five Commandments:

**Beat** → **Scene** → **Sequence** → **Act (Quadrant)** → **Subplot** → **Global Story**

- **Beat**: Smallest unit. An identifiable moment of change between two characters. The actor's medium.
- **Scene**: The building block. Must contain a definable VALUE SHIFT from beginning to end. If no shift, it's not a scene — it's just talk.
- **Sequence**: A "critical moment in life" comprising 2-7 scenes. Can be summed up with phrases like "Getting the Job" or "First Kiss." Results in an irreversible change.
- **Act (Quadrant)**: A major life stage. Act climaxes are IRREVERSIBLE — someone dies, someone gets pregnant, the world changes permanently. Must shift the global genre value.
- **Subplot**: Added attraction that amplifies theme or counterbalances with irony. Love story is the most common subplot genre. Subplots have their own Five Commandments.
- **Global Story**: The entire work. Five Commandments at the highest level.

## The 25/50/25 Structure and 15 Skeletal Scenes

For features, the global story breaks down:
- **Beginning Hook**: ~25% of runtime — Shock, Denial
- **Middle Build**: ~50% of runtime — Anger, Bargaining, Depression, Deliberation
- **Ending Payoff**: ~25% of runtime — Choice, Integration

Each section (BH, MB, EP) must contain its own complete Five Commandments. That gives you **15 Skeletal Scenes** minimum — the backbone of any story:

BH: Inciting Incident | Turning Point | Crisis | Climax | Resolution
MB: Inciting Incident | Turning Point | Crisis | Climax | Resolution
EP: Inciting Incident | Turning Point | Crisis | Climax | Resolution

## Controlling Idea (Theme)

The main takeaway — what the story is ABOUT at its deepest level. Every scene, every crisis, every choice should relate to it.

**Formula**: `[VALUE] prevails/fails when [PROTAGONIST ACTION]`
- **Prescriptive** (protagonist moves UP the value spectrum): "Justice prevails when the protagonist empathizes with the victims."
- **Cautionary** (protagonist moves DOWN): "Evil silences truth when we trade integrity for ambition."

To build: (1) Identify the Content Genre and its core value. (2) Choose prescriptive or cautionary. (3) Fill in the formula.

## A Story vs B Story

- **A Story (External)**: The visible quest for the conscious object of desire — PLOT
- **B Story (Internal)**: The hidden quest for the subconscious object of desire — THEME

The interplay creates meaning. Deepest stories: the protagonist achieves one but fails the other (irony). Example: The Silence of the Lambs — External Thriller (justice) + Internal Worldview Disillusionment.

## Conventions and Obligatory Moments

**Conventions** SET UP expectations — requirements for the world, avatars, and circumstances. Two types:
- **Selective Constraints** (Setting): Location, levels of conflict (inner, personal, extrapersonal)
- **Enabling Constraints** (Avatars & Catalysts): Protagonist, antagonist, mentor, shapeshifter, herald, threshold guardian, allies, trickster + catalysts (clock, MacGuffin, red herrings, point of no return)

**Obligatory Moments** PAY OFF those expectations — the key events producing the most pronounced value shifts. Readers recognize and remember these. They MUST be on stage.

## Narrative Path: POP, Narrative Device, and Point of View

**POP (Proposition of Possibility)**: The premise governing the story's problem space — four elements that generate and constrain what the story explores.

**Narrative Device**: Mental model of the Author communicating the POP to a Single Audience Member (SAM) to help solve a specific problem. This relevance filter determines which scenes belong.

**Point of View**: Three technical choices:
- **Person**: First, Second, or Third
- **Tense**: Past, Present, Future
- **Mode**: Objective (camera), Subjective (thoughts), Free Indirect Style (Omniscient Intracranial — wide-angle mind-reading from a single character's POV)

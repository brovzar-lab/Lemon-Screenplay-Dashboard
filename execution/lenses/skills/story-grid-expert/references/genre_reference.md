# Story Grid Genre Reference — All 11 Content Genres

Complete conventions, obligatory moments, Four Core Framework, skeletal scenes, and subgenres for each genre. Sourced from Shawn Coyne's Story Grid methodology.

## Table of Contents
- External Genres: Action | War | Horror | Crime | Thriller | Western/Eastern | Love | Performance | Society
- Internal Genres: Status | Morality | Worldview

---

## EXTERNAL GENRES

---

### ACTION

**Core Need**: Survival — the primal need to survive an inciting attack.
**Core Value**: Death → Life (with Damnation as negation of the negation)
**Core Emotion**: Excitement
**Core Event**: Hero at the Mercy of the Villain — the protagonist chooses whether to express their unique gifts by sacrificing to defeat the villain.
**Controlling Idea**: Life is preserved when the protagonist sacrifices to overpower or outwit the antagonist. Death results when courage is lacking.

**Conventions (9)**:
1. Intense, disturbed, unbalanced physical/social environment
2. Dueling hierarchies: growth hierarchy at odds with power/dominance hierarchy
3. Hero who sets out to stop the force of antagonism and save the victim
4. Villain much more powerful than hero and victim
5. Victim much less powerful, requires saving
6. Speech in praise of the villain — an avatar reveals why the villain appears unbeatable
7. Deadline/ticking clock
8. Set-piece action sequences showing protagonist strengths, weaknesses, new skills
9. Fast-paced, exciting plot with characters in extreme situations forced to take risks

**Obligatory Moments (8)**:
1. Inciting attack or threat by the villain
2. Hero sidesteps responsibility to take action
3. Forced to leave the ordinary world, the hero lashes out
4. Protagonist discovers and understands the antagonist's MacGuffin (villain's object of desire)
5. Hero's initial strategy against the villain fails
6. All Is Lost moment — hero realizes they must change approach to preserve life
7. Hero at the Mercy of the Villain — hero's gift is expressed (Core Event)
8. Hero's sacrifice is rewarded

**Skeletal Scenes**:
- BH: Inciting attack → Hero senses threat → Run away or engage? → Agrees to engage → Fix it and forget it mission
- MB1: Hero sees new world → Becomes the target → Comply or defy? → Chooses; antagonist asserts power → Point of no return
- MB2: Dropped into chaos → All Is Lost moment → How can death be meaningful? → Absolute commitment → Prepares for ultimate arena
- EP: Enters antagonist's sanctum → Someone the reader cares about dies → Do ends justify means? → Protagonist at mercy of antagonist → Rewarded

**Subgenres**:
- **Adventure** (Person vs. Nature): Labyrinth (Die Hard) | Monster (Jaws) | Environment (Gravity) | Doomsday (Independence Day)
- **Duel** (Person vs. Person): Revenge (The Count of Monte Cristo) | Hunted (The Most Dangerous Game) | Machiavellian (A Fistful of Dollars) | Collision (The Night Circus)
- **Epic** (Person vs. State): Rebellion (Star Wars) | Conspiracy (The Bourne Identity) | Vigilante (Daredevil) | Savior (The Dark Knight)
- **Clock** (Person vs. Time): Ransom (Romancing the Stone) | Countdown (The Andromeda Strain) | Holdout (300) | Fate (Back to the Future)

### WAR

**Core Need**: Safety — group survival when attacked.
**Core Value**: Dishonor → Honor (with life/death in play but not the core spectrum)
**Core Emotion**: Intrigue — intense fascination about how avatars contend with impossible situations.
**Core Event**: The Big Battle — the protagonist must choose between their own safety and the group's.
**Controlling Idea**: War derives meaning from noble love and self-sacrifice. It lacks meaning when leaders corrupt warriors' sacrifices.

**Conventions (5)**:
1. One central avatar with offshoot avatars embodying multiple personality traits
2. Big Canvas — wide-scope external setting or internal landscape
3. Overwhelming odds — protagonists substantially outnumbered
4. Clear point of no return — combatants accept the inevitability of death
5. Sacrifice for kinship moment — one protagonist sacrifices for fellow soldiers

**Obligatory Moments (8)**:
1. Inciting attack
2. Protagonists deny responsibility to respond
3. Forced to respond, protagonists lash out according to their positions in the power hierarchy
4. Each avatar learns what their antagonist's object of desire is
5. Protagonists' initial strategy fails
6. All Is Lost moment — must change approach
7. The Big Battle — core event; gifts expressed or withheld
8. Protagonists rewarded with at least one level of satisfaction

**Subgenres**: Pro-War (The Longest Day) | Anti-War (All Quiet on the Western Front) | Kinship of War (Gates of Fire)

### HORROR

**Core Need**: Safety — threatened by the monster, our deepest fears made manifest.
**Core Value**: Damnation → Life (damnation = fate worse than death, always in play)
**Core Emotion**: Fear — readers choose horror to experience courage without actual risk.
**Core Event**: Victim at the Mercy of the Monster — the victim summons courage and slays the monster, or sacrifices themselves.
**Controlling Idea**: Life is preserved when the ordinary person overpowers or outwits the monster. Death and damnation result when courage fails.

**Conventions (6)**:
1. Conventional settings within fantastical worlds — the familiar grounds the fantasy
2. Labyrinths — claustrophobic settings that conceal dangers
3. Monster cannot be reasoned with — possessed by evil, present to devour and annihilate
4. Perpetual discomfort — monster attacks randomly so the audience cannot settle
5. Mask the power of the monster — progressively reveal greater levels of power
6. Sadomasochistic flip-flop — let the reader experience the monster's power while empathizing with victims

**Obligatory Moments (6)**:
1. Inciting attack by the monster
2. Single non-heroic protagonist thrown out of stasis, forced to save their own life
3. Speech in Praise of the Monster — reveals the unbeatable nature of the monster
4. Protagonist becomes the final victim after a series of kill-off scenes
5. Victim at the Mercy of the Monster (Core Event) — victim unleashes their gifts
6. False Ending — there MUST be two endings

**Subgenres**: Uncanny (Misery, Get Out) | Supernatural (Dracula, The Amityville Horror) | Ambiguous (The Shining, Annihilation)

### CRIME

**Core Need**: Safety — crime threatens the security and coherence of society.
**Core Value**: Injustice → Justice (with tyranny as negation of the negation)
**Core Emotion**: Intrigue — desire to see the puzzle solved; satisfaction when the penny drops.
**Core Event**: Exposure of the Criminal — the protagonist must choose between their own safety and society's.
**Controlling Idea**: Justice prevails when the protagonist uses their gift to reveal truth. Tyranny reigns when the perpetrator outwits the system.

**Conventions (5)**:
1. MacGuffin — the antagonist's object of desire
2. Investigative Red Herrings — false clues misleading the investigator
3. Making It Personal — the antagonist manipulates the protagonist
4. Clock — limited time for protagonist or antagonist to act
5. Subgenre-specific conventions

**Obligatory Moments (6)**:
1. Inciting Crime — there must be victims
2. Speech in Praise of the Villain — the cunning of the villain revealed
3. Discovering and understanding the antagonist's MacGuffin — the key clue
4. Progressively complicated following of clues — investigator uncovers the antagonist's identity
5. Exposure of the Criminal (Core Event)
6. Brought to Justice or Escapes Justice — the global resolution

**Subgenres**:
- **Murder Mystery**: Master Detective | Cozy | Historical | Noir/Hardboiled | Paranormal | Police Procedural
- **Other Crime**: Organized Crime (Breaking Bad) | Caper (Ocean's Eleven) | Courtroom (Presumed Innocent) | Newsroom (All the President's Men) | Espionage (The Spy Who Came in from the Cold) | Prison (The Shawshank Redemption)

### THRILLER

**Core Need**: Safety — threatened by a master villain combining Action + Horror + Crime.
**Core Value**: Damnation → Life (the crime becomes personal; protagonist faces damnation)
**Core Emotion**: Excitement — affirms individual sovereignty when unique gifts defeat powerful antagonism.
**Core Event**: Hero at the Mercy of the Villain — the protagonist unleashes their unique gift.
**Controlling Idea**: Life is preserved when the protagonist unleashes their unique gift. Death or damnation triumphs when they fail.

Thriller is the HYBRID genre: powerful individual protagonist (Action) faces the embodiment of evil (Horror) in a story about unmasking a criminal (Crime).

**Conventions (4)**:
1. MacGuffin — the villain's object of desire
2. Investigative Red Herrings
3. Making It Personal — the villain wants not just to beat the protagonist but to make it painful
4. Clock — limited time for the protagonist to act

**Obligatory Moments (5)**:
1. Inciting Crime indicative of a master villain — there must be MULTIPLE victims
2. Speech in Praise of the Villain — revelation praising the villain's cunning
3. Protagonist becomes the Victim — villain makes the crimes personal
4. Hero at the Mercy of the Villain (Core Event) — protagonist unleashes their gift
5. False Ending — there MUST be two endings

**Subgenres**: Serial Killer | Medical | Legal | Psychological | Espionage | Child in Jeopardy | Military | Political | Journalism | Financial | Woman in Jeopardy | Hitchcock

### WESTERN / EASTERN

**Core Need**: Individual Sovereignty — the protagonist must transcend self-interest and sacrifice.
**Core Value**: Subjugation → Freedom
**Core Emotion**: Intrigue — a penny-drop revelation that sets the world right.
**Core Event**: The Big Showdown — the protagonist must choose between sacrificing their autonomy and subjugating themselves to save the community.
**Controlling Idea**: Justice prevails when an uncompromising individual sacrifices for others. Tyranny reigns if the individual is betrayed by those they defend.

**Conventions (8)**:
1. Harsh, hostile, wide-open landscape
2. Hero, victim, and villain clearly defined
3. MacGuffin — villain has an object of desire; protagonist stands in the way
4. Hero's object of desire: stop the villain and save the victim
5. Hero operates outside the law
6. Large power divide between hero and villain
7. Speech in praise of the villain
8. Subgenre-specific conventions

**Obligatory Moments (8)**:
1. Inciting attack by villain or environment
2. Hero sidesteps responsibility
3. Forced to leave the ordinary world, hero lashes out
4. Protagonist discovers antagonist's MacGuffin
5. Hero's initial strategy fails
6. All Is Lost moment — must change approach
7. The showdown or gunfight (Core Event)
8. Hero's sacrifice is rewarded — rides off or joins the town

**Subgenres**: Vengeance (Yojimbo) | Transition (High Noon) | Professional (True Grit, Crouching Tiger Hidden Dragon)

### LOVE

**Core Need**: Connection — yearning for intimacy and the assurance that another person sees and knows you.
**Core Value**: Hate → Love (negative extends to hate masquerading as love; positive branches into three subgenre tracks)
**Core Emotion**: Romance — felt when lovers sacrifice selflessly without hope it will do them good.
**Core Event**: Proof of Love — the most powerful moment; proves two together is exponentially stronger than one.
**Controlling Idea**: Love triumphs when lovers overcome moral failings or sacrifice for each other. Love fails when they don't evolve beyond desire.

**Conventions (8)**:
1. Triangle — a rival or personal ethic opposing the relationship
2. Helpers and Harmers — avatars radically for or against the relationship
3. Ordered and Chaotic Approaches — lovers have opposite approaches that integrate into a balanced whole
4. External Need — something outside the romance driving the main avatars
5. Opposing Forces — forces beyond or within avatars' control opposing the match
6. Secrets — four types: (1) society keeps from couple, (2) couple keeps from society, (3) couple keeps from each other, (4) a lover keeps from themselves (the character flaw preventing intimacy)
7. Rituals — shared traditions, private language, inside jokes
8. Moral Weight — those who cannot love have a moral failing they must overcome

**Obligatory Moments (6)**:
1. Lovers meet — become aware of one another
2. First Kiss or Intimate Connection — acknowledgment of care or attraction
3. Confession of love — one lover becomes vulnerable
4. Lovers break up — something forces them apart
5. Proof of Love (Core Event) — lovers sacrifice without promise it will do them good
6. Lovers reunite and commit — or don't

**Subgenres**: Obsession/Desire (The Great Gatsby — cautionary) | Courtship/Commitment (Pride and Prejudice — prescriptive) | Marriage/Intimacy (Marriage Story — ironic)

### PERFORMANCE

**Core Need**: Esteem — outward expression of internal gifts and the need for approval.
**Core Value**: Shame → Respect (tension: intrapersonal, interpersonal, and extrapersonal esteem are competing and mutually exclusive)
**Core Emotion**: Triumph
**Core Event**: The Big Performance Scene — unique gift is expressed.
**Controlling Idea**: We gain respect when we commit to expressing our gifts unconditionally. Shame results when we hold our gifts back for fear of criticism.

**Conventions (6)**:
1. Strong Mentor figure who challenges and guides
2. Training — protagonist must practice to gain or recover skills
3. Explicit All Is Lost moment
4. The mentor recovers their moral compass or betrays the protagonist
5. Wide and deep power divide between antagonist and protagonist
6. Win-but-lose or lose-but-win ending

**Obligatory Moments (8)**:
1. Inciting performance opportunity
2. Protagonist sidesteps responsibility to perform
3. Forced to perform, protagonist lashes out
4. Protagonist discovers antagonist's object of desire
5. Initial strategy to outmaneuver the antagonist fails
6. All Is Lost moment — must change approach
7. The Big Performance Scene (Core Event) — unique gift is expressed
8. Protagonist rewarded at one or more levels of satisfaction

**Subgenres**: Sports (Rocky) | Business/Profession (Hidden Figures) | Art (Mr. Turner) | Performing Arts (Whiplash, Billy Elliot)

### SOCIETY

**Core Need**: Recognition — disenfranchised group's need for agency and voice.
**Core Value**: Impotence → Power (with additional needs: justice, freedom, love, social mobility, or self-actualization)
**Core Emotion**: Intrigue — with triumph if the revolution succeeds, righteous indignation if it fails.
**Core Event**: The Revolution — a distinct shift in control from one segment of society to another.
**Controlling Idea**: We gain power when we expose the hypocrisy of tyrants. Tyrants beat back revolutions by co-opting the leaders of the underclass.

**Conventions (6)**:
1. One central character with offshoot characters embodying multiple personality traits
2. Big Canvas — wide-scope external or internal landscape
3. Clear revolutionary Point of No Return
4. The vanquished are doomed to exile
5. Large power divide between those in power and disenfranchised
6. Paradoxical win-but-lose or lose-but-win bittersweet ending

**Obligatory Moments (8)**:
1. A threat to or opportunity for the reigning power incites the protagonist
2. Protagonist denies responsibility to respond
3. Forced to respond, protagonist lashes out according to position in the power hierarchy
4. Each avatar learns what the antagonist's object of desire is
5. Protagonist's initial strategy fails
6. All Is Lost moment — change in approach required to turn the power tables
7. The Revolution Scene (Core Event) — protagonist's gifts expressed, power changes hands
8. Protagonist rewarded at the extrapersonal, interpersonal, or personal level

**Subgenres**: Single Protagonist/Arch-Plot (1984, Selma) | Multiple Protagonists/Mini-Plot (Animal Farm, Dead Poets Society) | First Party (family — Ordinary People) | Second Party (tribe — Dead Poets Society) | Third Party (mass group — Brave New World)

## INTERNAL GENRES

---

### STATUS

**Core Need**: Respect — seeking it from others without abandoning personal values.
**Core Value**: Failure → Success (negative end: "selling out"; positive: compromise on the way to success)
**Core Emotion**: Admiration (prescriptive) or Pity (cautionary)
**Core Event**: The Big Choice — achieve success through the traditional power hierarchy (selling out) or stay true to themselves.
**Controlling Idea**: Staying true to one's values defines success. Selling out for rank, praise, or acquisitions is failure.

**Conventions (6)**:
1. Strong Mentor Figure
2. Big Social Problem as subtext (racism, misogyny, poverty)
3. Shapeshifters as Hypocrites — say one thing, do another
4. Herald or Threshold Guardian — a fellow striver who sold out
5. Clear Point of No Return/Truth Will Out moment
6. Paradoxical Win-But-Lose or Lose-But-Win bittersweet ending

**Obligatory Moments (8)**:
1. Inciting opportunity or challenge
2. Protagonist leaves home (real or metaphorical) to seek their fortune
3. Forced to adapt to a new environment, protagonist relies on old habits and suffers humiliation
4. Protagonist learns what the external antagonist's object of desire is
5. Initial strategy to outmaneuver the antagonist fails
6. All Is Lost moment — must change their definition of success or risk betraying values
7. The Big Choice (Core Event) — achieve status through the hierarchy or reject that world
8. Protagonist saved or lost based on their action in the Big Choice

**Subgenres**: Pathetic (Little Miss Sunshine — weak protagonist falls) | Tragic (American Tragedy — gifted protagonist self-destructs) | Sentimental (Rocky, Brooklyn — weak protagonist succeeds against odds) | Admiration (Jane Eyre, Gladiator — principled protagonist rises without compromise)

### MORALITY

**Core Need**: Self-transcendence — an opportunity to contribute to the greater good after a life of self-serving survival.
**Core Value**: Selfishness → Altruism
**Core Emotion**: Satisfaction (prescriptive) or Contempt (cautionary)
**Core Event**: The Big Choice Scene — act for themselves or the greater good.
**Controlling Idea**: Prescriptive: We transcend selfishness when we share our gifts for others. Cautionary: We are damned when we selfishly withhold our gifts.

**Conventions (5)**:
1. Despicable protagonist begins at their worst
2. Spiritual mentor or sidekick illuminates aspects of the problem the protagonist cannot see
3. Seemingly impossible external conflict forces the choice to share or withhold gifts
4. Ghosts from protagonist's past torment them
5. Aid from unexpected sources

**Obligatory Moments (5)**:
1. A shock upsets the hibernating authentic self
2. Protagonist expresses inner darkness with overt refusal of the call to change
3. All Is Lost moment — protagonist recovers their inner moral code or chooses the immoral path
4. Protagonist actively sacrifices self in service of an individual, group, or humanity (prescriptive) OR consciously chooses to remain selfish (cautionary)
5. Protagonist faces literal or metaphorical death — either loses the battle but gains self-respect and meaning, or wins but loses what makes life meaningful

**Subgenres**: Punitive (Wall Street — unsympathetic protagonist suffers well-deserved misfortune) | Redemption (A Christmas Carol, Casablanca — begins doing wrong, ends making a better choice) | Testing—Triumph (Cool Hand Luke — pressured to compromise, remains steadfast) | Testing—Surrender (The Hurt Locker — formerly strong protagonist can't recover, resigns to weakness)

### WORLDVIEW

**Core Need**: Self-actualization — dismantling the current worldview to create a deeper one.
**Core Value**: Ignorance → Wisdom (through Naivete → Sophistication, with negation of the negation being blind certainty/meaninglessness)
**Core Emotion**: Satisfaction (prescriptive) or Pity (cautionary)
**Core Event**: Climax of the Worldview Shift — what does the protagonist do with the new wisdom available?
**Controlling Idea**: Prescriptive: We gain wisdom by sharing our gifts with an imperfect world. Cautionary: We descend into meaninglessness by clinging to black-and-white thinking.

**Conventions (5)**:
1. Mentor Figure who has achieved the wisdom the protagonist needs
2. Big Social Problem as subtext
3. Shapeshifters as Hypocrites — appear wise but are not
4. Clear Point of No Return — old worldview can no longer be held
5. Paradoxical Win-But-Lose or Lose-But-Win bittersweet ending

**Obligatory Moments (8)**:
1. Inciting opportunity or challenge
2. Protagonist denies responsibility to respond
3. When forced, protagonist lashes out against the requirement to change behavior
4. Protagonist learns what the antagonist's external object of desire is
5. Initial strategy to outmaneuver the antagonist fails
6. All Is Lost moment — must integrate the irony of reality into their worldview
7. Climax (Core Event) — protagonist's gifts expressed as acceptance of an imperfect world
8. Protagonist's loss of ignorance rewarded with deeper understanding

**Subgenres**: Maturation (To Kill a Mockingbird — naivete to sophistication) | Disillusionment (The Great Gatsby — belief to disillusionment) | Education (Educating Rita — meaninglessness to meaning) | Revelation (Oedipus Rex — ignorance to wisdom)

**Note**: Worldview Maturation is essentially the Heroic Journey 2.0. All stories have maturation built in, as every change arc requires the protagonist to shift their point of view.

# Story Grid Foolscap Guide

The Foolscap Global Story Grid — your entire story on one page. Plus the Kubler-Ross Change Curve, the 15 Skeletal Scenes framework, and a worked example from The Silence of the Lambs.

---

## The Foolscap Global Story Grid

A single legal-sized page containing the key global elements of the story. It's a quick reference that keeps writers on track and helps make story decisions at every level.

### The Foolscap Template

**TOP SECTION (Global Story Elements)**

```
GENRE:
  External Content Genre: _______________
  Internal Content Genre: _______________
  Five-Leaf Clover: Time / Structure / Style / Reality / Content

CONVENTIONS (list all must-haves for your chosen genre):
  1. _______________
  2. _______________
  (etc.)

OBLIGATORY MOMENTS (list all must-haves):
  1. _______________
  2. _______________
  (etc.)

NARRATIVE DEVICE:
  Author: _______________
  Single Audience Member (SAM): _______________
  Problem the SAM is trying to solve: _______________

POINT OF VIEW:
  Person: 1st / 2nd / 3rd
  Tense: Past / Present / Future
  Mode: Objective / Subjective / Free Indirect Style (Omniscient Intracranial)

PROTAGONIST:
  Object of Desire (Conscious/External Want): _______________
  Object of Desire (Subconscious/Internal Need): _______________

ANTAGONIST:
  Object of Desire: _______________ (must be mutually exclusive with protagonist's)

CONTROLLING IDEA:
  [Value] prevails/fails when [Protagonist Action]
  External: _______________
  Internal: _______________

GLOBAL VALUE PROGRESSION:
  External: [+] → [+/-] → [-] → [--]
  Internal: [+] → [+/-] → [-] → [--]
```

  Crisis (Best Bad Choice or Irreconcilable Goods): _______________

  Resolution: _______________

ENDING PAYOFF (25%):
  External value moves from: ___ → ___
  Internal value moves from: ___ → ___

## The Foolscap Checklist

Before declaring the Foolscap complete, verify:

- [ ] Global Content Genre (+ secondary genre if applicable) identified
- [ ] Five-Leaf Genre Clover filled: Time / Structure / Style / Reality / Content
- [ ] ALL Conventions listed and accounted for in the manuscript
- [ ] ALL Obligatory Moments listed and accounted for in the manuscript
- [ ] Narrative Device defined: Author + SAM + Problem
- [ ] POV chosen: Person + Tense + Mode
- [ ] Protagonist's Object of Desire defined (want + need)
- [ ] Antagonist's Object of Desire defined (mutually exclusive with protagonist's)
- [ ] Controlling Idea written as: [Value] prevails/fails when [Action]
- [ ] Three movements each have complete Five Commandments = 15 Skeletal Scenes minimum
- [ ] Global value progression makes sense across all three movements

## The 15 Skeletal Scenes

Every story needs at minimum 15 scenes — five in each movement. These are the skeleton. Everything else is muscle, skin, and hair.

```
BEGINNING HOOK (5 scenes):
  1. Scene that is the Inciting Incident of the beginning
  2. Scene(s) that progressively complicate the beginning
  3. Scene that creates the crisis question of the beginning
  4. Scene that climaxes the beginning
  5. Scene that resolves the beginning

MIDDLE BUILD (5 scenes):
  6. Scene that is the Inciting Incident of the middle
  7. Scene(s) that progressively complicate the middle
  8. Scene that creates the crisis question of the middle
  9. Scene that climaxes the middle
  10. Scene that resolves the middle

ENDING PAYOFF (5 scenes):
  11. Scene that is the Inciting Incident of the ending
  12. Scene(s) that progressively complicate the ending
  13. Scene that creates the crisis question of the ending
  14. Scene that climaxes the ending
  15. Scene that resolves the ending
```

### The Math for Features

For a 100-page screenplay (approx. 100 minutes):
- Beginning Hook: ~25 pages / ~25 minutes
- Middle Build: ~50 pages / ~50 minutes
- Ending Payoff: ~25 pages / ~25 minutes

If average scene length is 2-3 pages, you'll have approximately 35-50 scenes total. 15 are spoken for by the skeleton. The remaining 20-35 scenes are supporting scenes that build sequences and flesh out the story.

### The Math for TV Pilots

For a 60-minute pilot: BH ~15 min, MB ~30 min, EP ~15 min.
For a 30-minute pilot: BH ~7 min, MB ~15 min, EP ~8 min.

Adjust for act breaks — each commercial break should fall at or near a crisis point. In streaming (no commercial breaks), the BH/MB/EP structure still applies but transitions can be more fluid.

## The Kubler-Ross Change Curve for Story

Shawn Coyne maps the psychological stages of processing change (from Elizabeth Kubler-Ross's On Death and Dying) onto story structure. The protagonist's emotional journey through change IS the story:

```
BEGINNING HOOK (25%):
  SHOCK → DENIAL
  The protagonist's life is knocked off balance. They don't fully realize
  it yet. They pretend everything is normal until forced to face facts.

MIDDLE BUILD (50%):
  ANGER → BARGAINING → DEPRESSION → DELIBERATION
  Once they can no longer deny the situation:
  - ANGER: They blame others, lash out, making things worse
  - BARGAINING: They look for the easy way out, change environment, push the problem elsewhere
  - DEPRESSION: Bargaining fails. The monkey on their back can't be escaped
  - DELIBERATION: They finally face the crisis honestly. What must I do?

ENDING PAYOFF (25%):
  CHOICE → INTEGRATION
  - CHOICE: The climactic moment — the protagonist actively does something
    that metabolizes the Inciting Incident and changes their life forever
  - INTEGRATION: The new stability. They've come full circle with a new
    outlook. They're not the same person they were at the start.
```

### Using the Kubler-Ross Model

If you're stuck at any point in the story, ask: **Am I dramatizing the psychological turmoil of my lead character?**

- Are they in Shock? Show them not registering the change.
- In Denial? Show them going through motions as if nothing happened.
- In Anger? Show them lashing out — usually at the wrong targets.
- In Bargaining? Show them trying shortcuts and surface-level fixes.
- In Depression? Show the failure of bargaining, the weight of reality.
- In Deliberation? Show them honestly confronting the crisis question.
- At the Choice? Show them acting — ON STAGE, not reported.
- In Integration? Show the new equilibrium, how they've been transformed.

## Worked Example: The Silence of the Lambs

### Genre Classification
- External Content Genre: **Serial Killer Thriller**
- Internal Content Genre: **Worldview — Disillusionment**
- Five-Leaf: Long Form / Arch-Plot / Cinematic-Drama / Realism / Thriller + Worldview

### Global Value Progressions
- External (Thriller): Life → Unconsciousness → Death → Damnation
- Internal (Disillusionment): Blind Belief → Justified Belief → Doubt → Disillusionment

### Controlling Ideas
- External: Justice prevails when the protagonist empathizes with the victims
- Internal: We silence the word of God because the Devil's diction is far more entertaining / Evil silences truth
- Combined: Justice prevails when the protagonist engages her inner darkness as passionately as her positive side

### Point of View
Third person, past tense, Free Indirect Style (Omniscient Intracranial) — primarily Starling's POV with strategic shifts to Crawford, Gumb, Lecter, and others.

### Protagonist/Antagonist
- Starling's conscious Object of Desire: Work with Crawford as a certified FBI agent (catch Buffalo Bill)
- Starling's subconscious Object of Desire: Silence the screaming lambs — find peace by proving herself worthy through meritocracy
- Antagonist (Lecter): Wants freedom AND wants to psychoanalyze Starling — to crack her like a puzzle
- Antagonist (Buffalo Bill/Gumb): Wants metamorphosis — literally wants new skin

### Three Movements

**Beginning Hook** (Chapters 1-10, Scenes 1-12, ~19% of novel):
- External: Life → Unconsciousness (danger introduced but protagonist still alive/safe)
- Internal: Blind Belief → beginning of Justified Belief
- II: Crawford summons Starling for an "interesting errand" — interview Lecter
- TP: Starling's first visit to Lecter — the quid pro quo is established
- Crisis: Accept Lecter's dangerous game or protect herself and stay a plebe?
- Climax: Starling agrees to engage with Lecter
- Resolution: She's elevated — Crawford brings her along on the case

**Middle Build** (Chapters 11-48, Scenes 13-51, ~57% of novel):
- External: Unconsciousness → Death (bodies pile up, Catherine Martin abducted)
- Internal: Justified Belief peaks → crashes into Doubt → Disillusionment
- II: Discovery of the "floater" in West Virginia — third body; stakes escalate
- TP: Senator Martin's daughter Catherine is abducted — the clock starts ticking. Lecter's Faustian bargain deepens. Then: Chilton's betrayal, Lecter's escape, and the revelation that the FBI's meritocracy is a lie
- Crisis: Keep hunting Buffalo Bill and lose her place at the FBI? Or stay at Quantico and lament Catherine's death?
- Climax: With Crawford's blessing after Bella's death, Starling goes to Ohio alone — following Lecter's clues about empathizing with the first victim, Fredrica Bimmel
- Resolution: Starling is now operating under no illusions. She doesn't expect reward. She's a hero — sacrificing herself for the sake of others.

**Ending Payoff** (Chapters 49-61, Scenes 52-64, ~24% of novel):
- External: Death → Damnation threat → Life (barely)
- Internal: Full Disillusionment → acceptance of imperfect world
- II: Gumb moves to harvest Catherine's skin
- TP: Starling's investigation in Belvedere, Ohio — seeing the world through Fredrica's eyes leads her to Gumb's door
- Crisis: She's alone at Gumb's house. Go in or wait for backup that may come too late?
- Climax: Starling enters Gumb's basement — darkness, night vision goggles, the gun cocking — she kills him using skills Lecter taught her (goat smell, sound of the gun)
- Resolution: Catherine is saved. But Lecter is free. The lambs have stopped screaming — but the cost is real. Her disillusionment about the FBI and institutions is complete, but she's found her own moral compass.

### Key Structural Insight
Harris doesn't just write a thriller — he layers a Worldview Disillusionment plot that runs UNDERNEATH the external action. Starling's journey from blind belief in FBI meritocracy through justified belief, doubt, and ultimately disillusionment is what elevates the book from entertainment to art. The external and internal plots mirror each other: as the external danger escalates, so does her internal unraveling of illusions.

The transitional scenes between BH→MB and MB→EP are NOT big action scenes. They're value-shift scenes where Starling's relationship to truth changes. Harris trusts his story to deliver — not spectacle.

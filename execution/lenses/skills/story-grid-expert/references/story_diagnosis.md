# Story Grid Diagnostic Reference

Checklists, common problems, and troubleshooting procedures for screenplays that aren't working.

---

## The Four-Stage Editorial Process

### Stage 1: First Read
Read/watch the entire screenplay in one sitting without stopping. Record your gut reaction: Does it work? What moments landed? Where did you check out? Trust this first impression — it's the audience experience.

### Stage 2: Scene Inventory
Build the Story Grid Spreadsheet. Number every scene. Record Story Events, Word/Page Counts, and Value Shifts for the entire script. This is the diagnostic scan — like a CT scan of the story.

### Stage 3: Foolscap Analysis
Answer the Six Core Questions. Fill in the Foolscap Global Story Grid. Identify the three movements and their Five Commandments. This reveals macro-level structural problems.

### Stage 4: Story Grid Construction
Combine the Spreadsheet (micro) with the Foolscap (macro). Track external and internal value progressions scene by scene. Map the full story arc visually. This reveals the complete picture.

## Master Diagnostic Checklist

### Global Story Level

- [ ] **Genre is clear**: Can you name the External Content Genre in one word? If not, the story is confused.
- [ ] **Conventions present**: Every convention for the chosen genre is on the page. Not implied — ON THE PAGE.
- [ ] **Obligatory Moments present**: Every obligatory moment is dramatized on stage. Core Event is unmistakable.
- [ ] **Controlling Idea holds**: Can you state it in one sentence? Does every scene relate to it?
- [ ] **Global Inciting Incident works**: Upsets balance, contains the unknown, genre-specific, points to the ending.
- [ ] **Protagonist arcs**: Irrevocable change from beginning to end. Not the same person.
- [ ] **Antagonist is the dark mirror**: Shares the protagonist's gifts but made the opposite choice.
- [ ] **Objects of desire are mutually exclusive**: Protagonist and antagonist can't both win.
- [ ] **External and Internal genres complement each other**: A story + B story create ironic meaning.
- [ ] **Value progression reaches the end of the line**: The story goes to the Negation of the Negation before resolving.
- [ ] **25/50/25 structure holds**: BH, MB, and EP are proportional.
- [ ] **Three movements each have complete Five Commandments**: 15 Skeletal Scenes present.

### Scene Level

- [ ] **Every scene turns**: Clear value shift from beginning to end. No flat scenes.
- [ ] **Every scene has Five Commandments**: II, Progressive Complication, Crisis, Climax, Resolution.
- [ ] **Crisis questions are genuine dilemmas**: Best Bad Choice or Irreconcilable Goods — no easy outs.
- [ ] **Climaxes happen on stage**: The protagonist acts in front of the audience. Never reported.
- [ ] **Progressive complications escalate**: Each crisis is harder to reverse than the last.
- [ ] **Turning points are clear**: Every scene has an identifiable Action or Revelatory turning point.
- [ ] **Scenes serve the Controlling Idea**: Cut anything that doesn't. No matter how well-written.
- [ ] **POV is consistent and intentional**: Track who holds POV in each scene. Protagonist should dominate.

### Character Level

- [ ] **Protagonist is active**: Making choices, not just reacting. Passive protagonists kill stories.
- [ ] **Antagonist has a logical worldview**: They believe they're right. The audience can almost agree.
- [ ] **Cast represents multiple theme perspectives**: No two characters have the same relationship to the Controlling Idea.
- [ ] **Secondary characters serve a function**: Each one is an Ally, Enemy, Mentor, Shapeshifter, Herald, or Trickster — ideally a combination.
- [ ] **Characters are positioned across the Chaos/Order axis**: Friction is built into the cast structure.

## Common Story Problems and Diagnoses

### Problem: "It's boring"
**Diagnosis**: Progressive complications are flat. The story revisits the same level of conflict without escalating.
**Test**: Number the complication level of each scene from 1-10. If the numbers don't consistently increase toward the climax, the stakes aren't rising.
**Fix**: Make each crisis harder to reverse than the last. Push complications toward the limits of human experience — life, death, damnation.

### Problem: "The middle sags"
**Diagnosis**: The Middle Build is just filling time between Hook and Payoff. No real escalation of the global value.
**Test**: Can you identify the Kubler-Ross stages in the Middle Build? (Anger → Bargaining → Depression → Deliberation) If not, the emotional progression is missing.
**Fix**: The Middle Build must move the external value from the contrary level (-/+) to full negative (-). The internal value must peak and then crash. The protagonist's initial strategy MUST fail — don't let them succeed too easily or too early.

### Problem: "I don't care about the protagonist"
**Diagnosis**: The protagonist is passive, has no clear object of desire, or makes choices with no real stakes.
**Test**: For each scene, ask: What does the protagonist WANT right now? If the answer is unclear, the character lacks drive.
**Fix**: Give the protagonist a burning conscious desire (external genre-determined) AND a subconscious need they don't understand yet. Make the desires in tension — getting one should threaten the other.

### Problem: "The ending doesn't land"
**Diagnosis**: Usually one of three things: (a) the Ending Payoff doesn't reach the Negation of the Negation, (b) the climax happens offstage, or (c) the resolution is rushed.
**Test**: Does the EP take the global value to the absolute end of the line? Does the protagonist make their choice ON STAGE? Does the resolution create a genuinely new equilibrium — not just a summary?
**Fix**: Take the protagonist to the brink of the worst possible outcome (Negation of the Negation), make them choose in front of the audience, then let the resolution breathe. Don't dash off the ending.

### Problem: "It feels generic / like everything else"
**Diagnosis**: The obligatory moments and conventions are present but executed in the most obvious way possible.
**Test**: For each obligatory moment, ask: Is this the first thing anyone would think of? If yes, it's a cliché.
**Fix**: Genre demands WHAT must happen. Innovation is found in HOW it happens. Keep the structural function; change the execution. Study how masterworks in your genre surprised the audience while hitting every obligatory beat.

### Problem: "Scenes feel pointless"
**Diagnosis**: Scenes have no value shift. They're exposition or characters talking without conflict.
**Test**: For each scene, write a Scene Event Synthesis. If you can't, the scene has no turn.
**Fix**: Every scene needs a clear value at the beginning, a different value at the end, and a turning point (action or revelation) that causes the shift. If you can't create the shift, cut the scene and distribute its information elsewhere.

### Problem: "Too many subplots / it feels scattered"
**Diagnosis**: Subplots are competing with the global plot instead of serving it.
**Test**: For each subplot, ask: Does it amplify the Controlling Idea or create ironic counterpoint? If neither, it doesn't belong.
**Fix**: Build the global plot skeleton FIRST. Then weave in subplots that enhance theme. Cut subplots that divert from the Controlling Idea no matter how interesting they are in isolation.

### Problem: "The genre feels wrong"
**Diagnosis**: The writer is trying to write one genre but the story wants to be another — or the writer hasn't committed to a genre at all.
**Test**: What is the protagonist's conscious object of desire? That determines the External Content Genre. If the object of desire keeps shifting, the genre is unfocused.
**Fix**: Choose one External Content Genre and one Internal Content Genre. Commit. Make sure every convention and obligatory moment is accounted for. You can blend genre elements, but one must be primary.

### Problem: "It works but it's not great"
**Diagnosis**: The story hits all its genre obligations but lacks the Internal Content Genre depth that elevates entertainment to art.
**Test**: Is there a compelling B-story (Internal Genre) running underneath the A-story? Does the protagonist's inner transformation create ironic tension with the external outcome?
**Fix**: Layer an Internal Content Genre that creates ironic interplay with the External. The protagonist should achieve one objective but fail or complicate the other. This is how good stories become unforgettable ones. (See: The Silence of the Lambs — Thriller + Worldview Disillusionment.)

## The "Do No Harm" Principle

The first rule of editing: DO NO HARM. Most screenplays don't need the exhaustive depth of every Story Grid tool. Use the minimum diagnostic level needed to identify and fix the actual problems:

1. **If the gut read says it works** → Light touch. Check conventions/obligatory moments only.
2. **If specific scenes feel off** → Scene-level analysis (Scene Event Synthesis, Five Commandments check).
3. **If the middle sags or the ending doesn't land** → Foolscap analysis (macro structure, value progression).
4. **If nothing seems to work** → Full Story Grid Spreadsheet + Foolscap + diagnostic checklist.

Don't use the nuclear option on every screenplay. Save the deep analysis for deep problems.

## Quick Triage Questions

When first encountering a screenplay that might be broken, ask these five questions fast:

1. **What's the genre?** (If you can't answer in one word, that's the problem)
2. **What's the global Inciting Incident?** (If it's not in the first 10-15 pages, that's likely the problem)
3. **Does the protagonist make an active choice in the climax?** (If not, that's the problem)
4. **Can you identify the Core Event of the genre?** (If it's missing, that's the problem)
5. **Does the protagonist change irrevocably?** (If not, the story will feel hollow)

If the answer to any of these is unclear, you've found your starting point for diagnosis.

# Story Grid Scene Analysis Reference

Complete scene-level analysis tools: Scene Event Synthesis, the Story Grid Spreadsheet, value tracking, and diagnostic protocols for individual scenes.

---

## Scene Event Synthesis

The Scene Event Synthesis is a one-sentence summary that captures the essence of what changed in a scene. It's the single most important diagnostic tool at the scene level.

### Formula

```
[Ending value] when [protagonist] [on-the-surface climax action]
despite [crisis tradeoff]
after [above-the-surface initial strategy outcome].
```

### Example (Silence of the Lambs, Scene 1)

> A new investigation begins when Starling agrees to interview Lecter despite the risk to her career and safety after Crawford summons her with an "interesting errand."

### How to Use It

Write a Scene Event Synthesis for EVERY scene in the screenplay. If you cannot write one, the scene has no turn — it doesn't work. The synthesis forces you to identify the value shift, the climax action, the crisis at stake, and the causal chain.

## The Story Grid Spreadsheet — 14 Columns

The spreadsheet is the quantitative backbone of Story Grid analysis. It tracks every scene across the whole manuscript, making visual patterns visible — structural problems, missing obligatory moments, and value-shift imbalances become obvious at a glance.

### Column Reference

| # | Column | What to Record |
|---|--------|---------------|
| 1 | **Scene Number** | Sequential number (1, 2, 3...) |
| 2 | **Word Count / Page Count** | Length of the scene in words or screenplay pages |
| 3 | **Story Event** | One sentence or phrase capturing the gist of what happened. Reduce to essence. If you can't identify an event, the scene may be pure exposition and can be cut. |
| 4 | **Value Shift** | The value at stake at the beginning → the value at the end (e.g., "Life → Death", "Trust → Betrayal", "Ignorance → Knowledge") |
| 5 | **Polarity Shift** | Direction of the shift: + to -, - to +, + to ++, - to -- |
| 6 | **Turning Point** | The precise beat when the value shifts. Action (a character does something) or Revelation (new information changes everything). |
| 7 | **Point of View** | Which character's POV the scene is told from. Track to ensure the protagonist dominates and POV shifts are intentional. |
| 8 | **Period/Time** | Time of day, weekday, date, season. The more specific, the better for continuity. |
| 9 | **Duration** | How long the scene takes in story time (5 minutes, 3 hours, etc.) |
| 10 | **Location** | Where the scene takes place. Track for production and continuity. |
| 11 | **Onstage Characters** | Characters physically present in the scene. |
| 12 | **Offstage Characters** | Characters referenced but not present — important for subplot tracking. |
| 13 | **Five Commandments** | For each scene, identify: Inciting Incident / Turning Point / Crisis / Climax / Resolution. Mark any that are missing. |
| 14 | **Scene Event Synthesis** | The one-sentence summary per the formula above. |

### How to Build It

1. Read/watch the entire screenplay once without stopping
2. Go back and number every scene sequentially
3. Fill in columns 1-3 first (Scene, Page Count, Story Event) for the entire script
4. Then fill in columns 4-6 (Value Shift, Polarity, Turning Point) — this is where problems become visible
5. Then fill in columns 7-12 (POV, Time, Duration, Location, Characters) for continuity
6. Finally, fill in columns 13-14 (Five Commandments, Scene Event Synthesis) — the deepest analysis layer

### What to Look For

- **Scenes with no value shift**: These aren't scenes. They're exposition. Cut or combine.
- **Scenes that shift the same direction repeatedly**: The story is monotonous. It needs reversals.
- **Too many scenes from non-protagonist POV**: The audience is losing connection with the lead.
- **Scenes that don't serve the Controlling Idea**: These are digressions regardless of how well-written they are.
- **Scenes where you can't identify the Five Commandments**: The scene structure is broken.

## Value Tracking — Detailed Protocol

### The Four-Level Value Spectrum

Every genre has a core value that exists on a four-level spectrum. The story must progress through these levels to reach the "end of the line":

```
POSITIVE (+)  →  CONTRARY (+/-)  →  NEGATIVE (-)  →  NEGATION OF NEGATION (--)
```

The Negation of the Negation is the deepest level of human experience within the genre's value system. This is where the most compelling stories take their protagonists.

### Complete Value Spectrums by Genre

| Genre | + (Positive) | +/- (Contrary) | - (Negative) | -- (Negation of Negation) |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|--------------|--------------------------|
| **Action** | Life | Risk of Injury/Unconsciousness | Death | Damnation (fate worse than death) |
| **Horror** | Life | Risk of Injury/Unconsciousness | Death | Damnation |
| **Thriller** | Life | Risk of Injury/Unconsciousness | Death | Damnation |
| **Crime** | Justice | Unfairness | Injustice | Tyranny (injustice disguised as justice) |
| **Western** | Freedom | Compromise | Subjugation | Subjugation disguised as freedom |
| **War** | Honor/Victory | Compromise | Defeat/Dishonor | Dishonor disguised as honor |
| **Love** | Love | Desire/Attraction | Indifference/Hate | Self-hatred masquerading as love |
| **Performance** | Respect/Esteem | Recognition | Shame | Shame disguised as esteem |
| **Society** | Power/Agency | Influence | Impotence | Impotence disguised as power |
| **Status** | Success (true) | Compromise | Failure | Selling out (failure disguised as success) |
| **Morality** | Altruism | Self-interest | Selfishness | Self-destruction disguised as virtue |
| **Worldview** | Wisdom | Knowledge | Ignorance | Blind certainty (ignorance disguised as wisdom) |

### Global Value Progression Rules

1. **Beginning Hook**: Move the global value one level (e.g., Life → Unconsciousness)
2. **Middle Build**: Move it further (e.g., Unconsciousness → Death)
3. **Ending Payoff**: Take it to the end of the line (e.g., Death → Damnation threat, then resolution)
4. **Act climaxes MUST turn on the global genre value** — not secondary values
5. Individual scenes CAN turn on secondary values, but the big moments must turn on the global value
6. Valid scene-level polarity shifts include: +/-, -/+, +/++, and -/-- (escalation is essential)

## Scene-Level Analysis — Key Questions

For each scene, systematically ask:

1. **What is the value at the beginning? At the end?** (If the same — not a scene)
2. **What is the protagonist's initial strategy above the surface?** (What are they trying to do?)
3. **What turning point forces a change in strategy?** (Action or Revelation?)
4. **What is the crisis choice?** (Best Bad Choice or Irreconcilable Goods? If neither — the crisis is broken)
5. **What does the climax reveal about the protagonist's character?** (Actions define character, not words)
6. **How does the resolution pay off the crisis stakes?** (Don't just sum up — create a new equilibrium)
7. **Does the scene serve the Controlling Idea?** (If not, why is it here?)
8. **Is this scene's complication level appropriate for its position in the story?** (Early = more reversible; Late = less reversible)

## Scene Types and Their Functions

### Scenes That Must Be On Stage (Never Reported Offstage)
- All Obligatory Moments for the chosen genre
- Every protagonist climax — the audience must witness the choice and action
- The global Inciting Incident
- The Core Event of the genre

### Scenes That Can Be Off Stage
- Secondary character decisions (reported as revelations later)
- Setup/logistics scenes (collapse into the next active scene)
- Backstory (deliver through subtext, not exposition)

### The Cliché Test
The first thing that comes to mind for any scene is a cliché — it's all the stories pumped into your brain since childhood. Use cliché scenes as mile markers in your first draft to know the TYPE of scene you need. Then in revision, innovate the execution while keeping the structural function intact.

## Tracking Subplots

Subplots are tracked on the same spreadsheet with additional notation:
- Mark which subplot each scene belongs to (A-story, B-story, C-story, etc.)
- Subplots must have their own complete Five Commandments
- The most common subplot genre is Love Story (softens violent/horrific global stories)
- Subplots amplify theme or provide ironic counterbalance
- Subplots often emerge from the writer's subconscious — pay attention to these; they're usually right

### Subplot Integration Rule
Prioritize the global plot first. Drop in subplot payoffs after the global plot skeleton is built. Writers who let subplots overtake the global plot lose narrative momentum.

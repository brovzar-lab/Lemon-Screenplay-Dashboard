# Story Grid Character & Worldbuilding Reference

Protagonist/antagonist construction, the Three Axes of Character, archetypes, and worldbuilding guidelines.

---

## Protagonist and Antagonist

### Defining the Protagonist
The protagonist is the avatar whose life is knocked out of balance by the Inciting Incident AND who acts on the crucial choice in the Climax. Both conditions must be met — a character who is affected but never makes the final choice is not the protagonist.

### Defining the Antagonist
The antagonist is the protagonist's dark mirror — they made the OPPOSITE choice earlier in their life. Same gifts, same circumstances, different decision. This is why the best antagonists feel inevitable — they represent who the protagonist could become if they choose wrong.

### The Mirror Principle
- Protagonist and antagonist share the same core archetype expressed in opposite ways
- What separates them is not ability but choice — specifically, moral choice
- The most compelling stories have protagonists and antagonists who are "polar expressions of a single archetype"
- Example: Clarice Starling and Jame Gumb in The Silence of the Lambs — both want metamorphosis, new skin, to escape their origins. Starling pursues it through service and sacrifice; Gumb pursues it literally and violently

### Objects of Desire — Mutually Exclusive
The protagonist's and antagonist's objects of desire MUST be mutually exclusive. If both can get what they want simultaneously, there's no conflict. Only one can win.

- Protagonist's Conscious Object of Desire (External Want) — determined by the External Content Genre
- Protagonist's Subconscious Object of Desire (Internal Need) — determined by the Internal Content Genre
- Antagonist's Object of Desire — must directly oppose the protagonist's

### Character Arc
A character arc is the irrevocable change from beginning to end. Without it, no one will deeply care about the story. The arc manifests through the progression of crisis choices — each choice reveals more of who the character truly is.

- **Arch-Plot**: Protagonist MUST arc — irrevocable change from beginning to end
- **Mini-Plot**: Change may be subtler, internal, distributed across multiple characters
- **Anti-Plot**: No arc required (but audience appeal decreases)

## The Three Axes of Character

Every character exists on three axes simultaneously. A well-constructed cast has characters positioned across all three to create natural conflict and explore theme from multiple angles.

### Axis 1: Ally / Enemy
Every character either helps or hinders the protagonist's journey toward the story's truth. These roles are NOT fixed — a character can be an Ally in one scene and an Enemy in the next, not from inconsistency, but because they pursue their own truth.

The most interesting characters are BOTH — allies whose help comes at a cost, or enemies who inadvertently push the protagonist toward growth.

### Axis 2: Chaos / Order / Complexity
Characters fall on a spectrum from pure Chaos to pure Order:

- **Chaos characters** resist structure, break rules, destabilize situations, act on impulse, follow desire over duty
- **Order characters** enforce structure, follow rules, stabilize situations, act on principle, follow duty over desire
- **Complexity characters** hold both impulses in tension — they can move fluidly between chaos and order depending on context

Pairing chaos and order characters creates natural friction. The protagonist often begins at one extreme and must integrate the other — moving toward complexity.

### Axis 3: Theme Perspective
Each character has a unique relationship to the story's Controlling Idea — a different answer to the question the story is exploring.

A complete cast includes:
- The character who embodies the initial lie deeply (often the antagonist)
- One who already believes the truth but takes it too far (mentor or zealot)
- One who has learned the truth through experience (wise elder, damaged survivor)
- One who wants to believe but is terrified (the protagonist, often)

No two characters should have the same theme perspective. Each one argues for their position through their actions and choices.

## Character Archetypes

Story Grid uses a functional archetype system — archetypes defined by their ROLE in the story's progression:

### Primary Archetypes

**Protagonist**: Life knocked out of balance; must make the crucial climactic choice. Every beat, scene, sequence, act, and global story is measured against how it advances or complicates the protagonist's journey.

**Antagonist**: Opposes the protagonist's object of desire. The antagonist doesn't have to be a villain — just a force that makes the protagonist's goal harder to achieve. The best antagonists believe they're the hero of their own story.

### Supporting Archetypes

**Mentor**: Has achieved the wisdom or skill the protagonist needs. Challenges assumptions. May betray or be betrayed. In Performance stories, the mentor is a major convention. In Worldview and Status stories, the mentor embodies the truth the protagonist must discover.

**Shapeshifter/Hypocrite**: Says one thing, does another. Key convention in Status, Worldview, and Morality genres. Shapeshifters test the protagonist's ability to see through masks — a skill they'll need for the climax.

**Herald/Threshold Guardian**: A fellow striver who already sold out (in Status stories) or someone who announces the call to adventure/change. They represent the road not taken — or the road the protagonist must avoid.

**Allies**: Support the protagonist but have their own agendas. Best allies create secondary conflict even while helping. In Love stories, these are Helpers — avatars radically FOR the relationship.

**Trickster**: Disrupts the status quo through humor, deception, or chaos. Forces both protagonist and antagonist to adapt. Can be allied with either side or neutral.

**Harmers** (Love stories specifically): Avatars radically AGAINST the relationship — ex-lovers, disapproving parents, social prejudice embodied in specific characters.

## Building a Cast

### The Litmus Test
For each character, ask:
1. What is their unique perspective on the Controlling Idea?
2. What is their Object of Desire? (Must create conflict with at least one other character)
3. Where do they fall on the Chaos/Order spectrum?
4. Are they an Ally or Enemy to the protagonist — or both?
5. What archetype function do they serve?
6. Could two characters be combined without losing thematic coverage?

### Common Cast Problems
- **Too many characters with the same theme perspective**: Combine or cut
- **Characters who agree with the protagonist on everything**: Add friction or remove
- **Antagonist who is purely evil with no logic**: Give them a coherent worldview
- **Secondary characters who don't serve the Controlling Idea**: They may belong in a different story
- **No character who embodies the truth the protagonist needs**: Add a mentor or truth-teller

## Worldbuilding

Worldbuilding is the creation of the Alternate World — the complete setting and context for the story. Even realistic settings require deliberate worldbuilding.

### Three Things to Consider

1. **What are the rules of this world?** Every world has rules — physics, social norms, power structures, economics, technology. The rules must be established early and followed consistently. Breaking your own rules destroys trust.

2. **How does this world create conflict?** The setting should generate conflict organically. A story set in a claustrophobic submarine creates different conflict than one in an open desert. Choose settings that pressure the protagonist's specific weakness.

3. **How does this world express the Controlling Idea?** The world itself should be a metaphor for the theme. The decaying mansion in a Horror story, the cutthroat corporate hierarchy in a Status story, the lawless frontier in a Western — the world argues for the theme before any character opens their mouth.

### Three Common Worldbuilding Problems

1. **Under-building**: The world feels generic because the writer didn't make specific choices. Fix: add concrete, sensory details that only THIS world would have.

2. **Over-building**: The writer spends so much time on world mechanics that the story stalls. Fix: only include world details that create conflict or advance the plot. Save the rest for yourself.

3. **Inconsistency**: The rules change to serve plot convenience. Fix: establish rules early, test every plot point against them, and if a rule must be broken, make the breaking of it a major story event with consequences.

### Worldbuilding for Film/TV

In screenwriting, worldbuilding must be SHOWN, not told. You have no narrator to explain the rules. Instead:
- Open with an image or sequence that establishes the world's tone, rules, and power dynamics in the first 2-3 minutes
- Use character behavior to reveal social rules (how people greet each other, who defers to whom, what's taboo)
- Use production design, wardrobe, and location to express the world's theme without dialogue
- Introduce world rules through conflict — the audience learns what's forbidden when someone breaks a rule and faces consequences

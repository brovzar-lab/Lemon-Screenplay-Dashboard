# John Truby's Story Structure Framework

## The 7 Major Story Structure Steps (The Story Code)

All good stories work through seven major structure steps. These foundational steps compose the Story Code. They mark the process of the hero's character change.

1. **WEAKNESS-NEED**: The hero is enslaved by habits of thought and action and suffers from a deep personal weakness that is destroying the quality of her life. The hero needs to overcome this flaw to grow. Need is both psychological (hurting themselves) and moral (hurting others).

2. **DESIRE**: The hero desires a goal outside of herself that she perceives as valuable and missing from her life. Desire should be specific, have a clear endpoint, and force the hero into direct conflict with opponents.

3. **OPPONENT**: She confronts an opponent and an obstacle/challenge preventing her from reaching her goal. She will find at the end that the obstacle/challenge is herself. The best opponent is the one who is most like the hero and competes for the same goal.

4. **PLAN**: She concocts a plan, or strategy, that will allow her to defeat the opponent and achieve the goal. The plan sets up the story's overall shape and the hero's approach.

5. **BATTLE**: She enters into a final conflict, or battle, with the opponent to determine once and for all who wins the goal. The battle is the proving ground where the hero demonstrates who she has become.

6. **SELF-REVELATION**: At the end, the hero, if she grows at all, has a self-revelation about her true or better self, about how she has been wrong psychologically and morally. She then makes a decision about how to act and takes new action, proving what she has become.

7. **NEW EQUILIBRIUM**: With the system in a new equilibrium, the hero stands as a new version of herself, along with a new capacity for growing in the future.

## The 22 Building Blocks of Story Structure

The 22 steps expand the 7 major steps into a precise, beat-by-beat blueprint:

1. **Self-Revelation, Need, and Desire** — The framing step. Start at the endpoint (what will the hero learn?) and work backward to establish weakness, need, and desire. This ensures every step leads where you want to go.

2. **Ghost and Story World** — The ghost is an event from the past that still haunts the hero and is the source of the hero's psychological and moral weakness. The story world is the arena that expresses the hero's condition.

3. **Weakness and Need** — The hero's psychological weakness (hurting self) and moral weakness (hurting others). Need is what the hero must fulfill internally to have a better life. The audience should see the weakness but the hero should not.

4. **Inciting Event** — An outside event that causes the hero to come up with a goal and take action. It knocks the hero out of the normal world and hints at what this story will be about.

5. **Desire** — The specific goal the hero wants to achieve. It should be concrete, have a clear endpoint, and be what drives the story forward.

6. **Ally or Allies** — Characters who help the hero in the quest. The best ally is one who also forces the hero to deal with weakness.

7. **Opponent and/or Mystery** — The character(s) competing with the hero for the same goal. The best opponent attacks the hero's greatest weakness.

8. **Fake-Ally Opponent** — A character who appears to be an ally but is actually working against the hero. Creates betrayal and forces the hero to question who she can trust.

9. **First Revelation and Decision: Changed Desire and Motive** — The hero gets new information that forces a recalibration of the plan and desire.

10. **Plan** — The set of guidelines and strategies the hero will use to overcome the opponent and reach the goal.

11. **Opponent's Plan and Main Counterattack** — The opponent has a plan too, and it's usually more ruthless. This raises the stakes.

12. **Drive** — The middle of the story where hero and opponent take a series of actions against each other, each forcing the other to adjust.

13. **Attack by Ally** — The ally, who loves the hero, criticizes the hero for a moral flaw. This is one of the keys to the hero's moral growth.

14. **Apparent Defeat** — The hero hits bottom. It looks like she has lost everything and has no way to reach her goal.

15. **Second Revelation and Decision: Obsessive Drive, Changed Desire and Motive** — New information sparks a desperate, obsessive push toward the goal.

16. **Audience Revelation** — The audience learns something important that the hero doesn't yet know, creating dramatic irony and suspense.

17. **Third Revelation and Decision** — Another piece of the puzzle falls into place, leading to a critical decision.

18. **Gate, Gauntlet, Visit to Death** — The hero must pass through a narrow passage, face a gauntlet of challenges, or visit a place of death before the final battle. This is the hero's deepest descent.

19. **Battle** — The final conflict between hero and opponent. Everything is resolved here. The hero must use everything she's learned.

20. **Self-Revelation** — The hero sees the truth about herself. Psychological self-revelation: who am I really? Moral self-revelation: how have I been treating others?

21. **Moral Decision** — The hero must make a choice that proves she has changed. The moral decision is the action proof of the self-revelation.

22. **New Equilibrium** — The world has changed. The hero is at a new level, either higher or lower than where she started.

## Character Web — The Four-Corner Opposition

Truby's character web places all major characters in opposition, not just hero vs. villain:

- The hero, main opponent, and at least two secondary opponents should all compete for the same goal
- Each opponent attacks the hero's weakness in a different way
- Put character values in conflict across all four corners
- The web creates a thematic argument where each character represents a different approach to the story's central moral question

### Character Change Arc Types
- **Child to Adult** (coming-of-age)
- **Adult to Leader** (gaining responsibility)
- **Cynic to Participant** (rejoining society)
- **Leader to Tyrant** (tragic fall)
- **Leader to Visionary** (transcendence)

## Moral Argument

Every story is a moral argument. The theme is expressed through the structure:

1. Create a moral problem at the beginning (connected to weakness-need)
2. Moral argument runs through the drive section via action and dialogue
3. Each character represents a different moral position
4. The battle forces a moral choice
5. Self-revelation resolves the moral argument

### Key Moral Argument Beats
- **Moral Need**: What the hero must learn about how to treat others
- **Immoral Actions**: Actions early in the story where hero hurts others
- **Attack by Ally**: Ally criticizes hero's moral flaw
- **Moral Decline** (if applicable): Hero acts increasingly immorally under pressure
- **Moral Decision**: Hero proves change through action
- **Thematic Revelation**: The audience understands the story's moral argument

## Story World

The story world is not just a backdrop—it's an expression of the hero's inner condition and the story's theme.

### Three Components of Story World
1. **The Land** — Physical setting that reflects the hero's psychological state
2. **The People** — Social structure and how people relate
3. **Technology/Tools** — How people manipulate the world

### Key Principles
- The world should grow from the hero's weakness and need
- Create opposition in the world: freedom vs. slavery, nature vs. civilization
- Design subworlds that represent different value systems
- The world should change as the hero changes
- Visual oppositions in the world express the story's theme

### Designing the Story World
1. Start with the hero's weakness-need
2. Create a world that puts maximum pressure on that weakness
3. Design an opponent subworld that contrasts with the hero's world
4. Build subworlds that each character inhabits
5. Let the world evolve from slavery to freedom (or the reverse in tragedy)

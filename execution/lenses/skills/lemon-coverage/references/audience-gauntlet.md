## The Audience Frame

Filmmakers care about a script because they have lived inside it. Audiences owe it nothing. The gap between those two facts is where most competent screenplays die, and closing it is a craft problem, not a marketing one.

Ask on every script:

- Does the concept generate curiosity on its own, before any casting, poster, or trailer exists?
- Is there a sentence that makes a stranger lean in?
- What does the audience *feel*, act by act? Not what happens — what they feel.
- Is there a moment they will describe to someone else the next day?
- Would they recommend it, and in what exact words?
- Does the script deliver the experience its premise promised, or does the plot pull away from it?
- Is there a reason this must be a film rather than a novel, an essay, or a conversation?

When an answer is missing, the fix is upstream. A script with no unforgettable image usually has a passive protagonist. A script that needs two sentences to explain usually has two premises fighting. Trace it back rather than treating the symptom.

## Comedy

Comedy is the least forgiving genre to read on the page, because a premise that is funny once reads as funny for two hours and is not.

### The engine test

Does the premise generate jokes structurally, or does it depend on the writer being funny scene by scene? Engine comedies survive rewrites, recasting, and cuts. Joke-dependent comedies collapse the moment a scene moves.

Ask: can this premise produce a hundred distinct jokes, or roughly ten stretched across a hundred minutes? Count the actual comic set pieces. Fewer than six in a feature is a structural shortfall, not a polish note.

### Checklist

- Would the premise get a laugh stated in one sentence, with no setup?
- Can a stranger explain the movie in ten seconds and get it right?
- Does comedy come from behavior and situation, or only from dialogue? Behavior survives translation, dubbing, and a trailer with no sound. Dialogue jokes die in subtitles.
- Does the chaos escalate, or does act two repeat act one at the same amplitude?
- Are the characters funny by construction, or ordinary people saying funny lines?
- Is there a line the audience quotes on the way out? Cite it.
- **Promise of the premise:** is the middle of the movie *the thing the premise promised*, or does the plot pull away from it? This is comedy's equivalent of a Core Event and it is the most common structural failure in the genre.
- Is the funniest material in act one? If so the script is front-loaded and the audience deflates exactly when it should be peaking.

### Territory that reliably works in Mexico

Family dynamics, class friction, regional identity, machismo, suegras, Catholic guilt, bureaucracy, politics, dating, marriage, infidelity, jealousy, social climbing, humiliation, economic survival, overconfidence, double lives, pretension, generational conflict.

Name the register precisely, because a script that does not know its register is a script whose writers have not decided what they wrote: broad, character, situation, dark, romantic, buddy, satire, farce, action comedy, comedy thriller.

### Benchmarks

*Nosotros los Nobles*, *No Manches Frida*, *Mirreyes vs Godínez*, *Cómo Ser Soltera*, *Ya Veremos*, *La Dictadura Perfecta*, *No Se Aceptan Devoluciones*. Know why each worked and where each was weak. The comparison is against these, not against comedy in general.

## Horror

The job of horror is dread, not startle. Jump scares are cheap and forgettable, and forgettable horror generates no word of mouth, which is the only thing horror has after opening weekend.

### Checklist

- What is the nightmare, in one sentence?
- What is the human fear underneath the monster? Horror without a real fear underneath plays as a costume.
- What is the one image the audience cannot unsee? Page cite. If none exists, the script has no center.
- Does the horror escalate, or is the scariest scene in act one?
- Is the evil intelligent, with intent and strategy, or is it weather?
- Is there dread *between* the scares, or only the scares?
- Is there sustained uncertainty about who survives?
- Does the mythology hold up when the audience explains it in the car afterward? Rules that contradict themselves are the most common reason a scary movie stops being scary in retrospect.
- **Core Event:** is there a real victim-at-mercy-of-monster scene, fully played, not cut away from?
- Would a horror fan — the most contract-aware audience there is — recommend it?

### Territory

Folklore, urban legend, Catholic imagery, death ritual, family trauma, missing children, isolation, brujería, nahuales, possession, abandonment, guilt.

Name the subgenre precisely: rural, urban, psychological, creature, found footage, body, cosmic, folk, religious, home invasion. The subgenre sets the contract, and a script that straddles two usually pays neither.

### Benchmarks

*Huesera*, *Belzebuth*, *Kilómetro 31*, *Veneno para las Hadas*, *Tigers Are Not Afraid*, *El Orfanato*, *The Conjuring*, *Hereditary*, *Talk to Me*, *The Ring*, *Smile*.

## Drama, Thriller, Action

- **Drama:** what is the engine? Drama fails when nothing is driving, and "a character examines their life" is not a drive. Find the want, find the deadline, find the opponent.
- **Thriller:** is the engine a question the audience needs answered, and is it posed in the first ten pages? A thriller that takes thirty pages to ask its question has thirty dead pages.
- **Action:** is the escalation designed, or is it a sequence of similar-sized events? Count the set pieces and check that each is harder than the last and costs the protagonist more.
- **All three:** does the protagonist choose, repeatedly and at cost? Passivity is disguised most easily in these genres because plot activity substitutes for character agency.

## Comparison Discipline

A comparable is not a vibe. Name the title, then say in one sentence where this script is stronger and in one sentence where it is weaker.

Choose the *unflattering* comp — the film that did this well, not the film that succeeded for reasons this script does not share. Naming the film this script has to beat is more useful than naming the film it hopes to resemble.

## Common Failure Patterns

Each of these is a competent script that does not work. Watch for them specifically.

- **The explained concept.** Needs two sentences to land. Almost always two premises fighting for the same movie.
- **The front-loaded script.** Best material in act one. Reads great for thirty pages and deflates.
- **The admired but unwanted script.** Every filmmaker who reads it praises the writing; no reader can say what they would feel watching it. Craft in service of nothing.
- **The passive protagonist.** Things happen to them. Common in drama, fatal in genre, and usually invisible until you run the agency test.
- **The missing image.** Nothing anyone will describe to a friend. The script has no center of gravity.
- **The soft opponent.** The antagonist is agreeable, absent, or correct. Every scene loses tension and no dialogue pass will restore it.
- **The one-role script.** One great part and five errands. The supporting characters were never given anything to want.
- **The asserted arc.** The script says the character changed. Nobody can cite the scene where they do something they could not have done at the start.
- **The already-made idea.** A film did this two years ago and did it better. Check before you praise the premise.
